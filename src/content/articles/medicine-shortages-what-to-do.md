A medicine can be out of stock for two quite different reasons. Sometimes there's a national shortage, which the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) tracks and publishes. Other times, a medicine is briefly unavailable at one pharmacy or in one area while new stock works its way through the supply chain.

Either way, the TGA's advice is to speak to your doctor or pharmacist. Ask early, and keep taking your medicine while you sort it out. Here's why shortages happen, how to check for one, and what a pharmacist can and can't do.

## Why do medicines go out of stock?

The TGA explains that shortages happen when there isn't enough of a medicine in Australia to meet patient needs. It lists causes such as:

- manufacturing delays
- supply chain issues
- regulatory barriers and trade limitations
- unexpected spikes in demand

The TGA's 2024 shortages report says manufacturing issues are the most commonly reported reason. It also describes medicine shortages as a global problem, and says some can't be avoided.

### How common are shortages?

Quite common. Reporting medicine shortages has been mandatory in Australia since 1 January 2019. In 2019 alone, 1,797 new shortages were reported to the TGA, and its 2024 report says the number reported has been fairly consistent since.

That number sounds alarming, but it isn't the whole story. The TGA notes that each listed shortage refers to a single product, and alternative brands, strengths or dose forms are often available.

## Why can a medicine be in stock one day and gone the next?

Not every gap is a national shortage. The TGA says local supply disruptions can happen, where a medicine is unavailable in a particular pharmacy or area. In many cases, it says, these are resolved quickly once the wholesaler and pharmacy receive new stock.

A couple of other things can make stock come and go:

- **A sudden rush.** The TGA says local disruptions are sometimes caused by unexpected increases in demand.
- **Stock still on its way.** When a national shortage ends, the TGA notes it may take a week from the resolution date for stock to reach pharmacy shelves.

## How can I check if my medicine is in short supply?

The TGA lists national shortages in its [Medicine Shortage Reports Database](https://www.tga.gov.au/safety/shortages/medicine-shortage-reports-database). You can search it yourself: type in the active ingredient or the brand name on your box, then click the name for details.

It helps to know what the database covers:

- **National shortages only.** These are cases where supply isn't likely to meet normal demand in Australia within the next six months.
- **Not local problems.** Local supply disruptions aren't included, because there's still enough of the medicine in Australia overall.
- **Not most over-the-counter products.** The TGA notes most non-prescription products don't have to be reported.
- **What's being done.** The database also shows the TGA's management actions, including the supply of overseas alternative medicines.

If your medicine isn't listed but you still can't get it, a local supply problem is more likely. Ask your pharmacist about your options.

## What can a pharmacist do in a shortage?

The TGA says your health professionals are experienced in finding suitable options when a medicine is in short supply. It also notes that even during a shortage, a medicine may still be available from your pharmacy.

### Order it from another wholesaler

We work with several wholesalers and can source most medicines, including hard-to-find ones.

### Offer another brand

If another brand with the same active ingredient is available, it's your choice whether to take it. The exception is a script your doctor has marked "brand substitution not permitted". Our guide to [generic and brand-name medicines](/generic-vs-brand-medicines/) explains why it may look different.

### Substitute under an SSSI

For some serious shortages, the TGA puts a Serious Scarcity Substitution Instrument (SSSI) in place. It lets community pharmacists substitute specific medicines without first getting the prescriber's approval, so treatment isn't interrupted. SSSIs only cover prescription-only medicines, not controlled drugs or over-the-counter medicines.

Under an SSSI, the TGA says a pharmacist can substitute only when:

- you have a valid prescription for the scarce medicine
- the pharmacist can't get the scarce medicine
- your prescriber hasn't marked the script to say substitution isn't permitted
- the pharmacist has used their professional judgement to decide you're suitable
- you, or your carer, agree to the substitute
- the total quantity matches what you would have received of the original

The pharmacist must also record the substitution and let your prescriber know.

A pharmacist can't use an SSSI to:

- swap to any medicine that isn't named in the SSSI
- substitute when the scarce medicine is in stock
- substitute after the SSSI has ended or been revoked
- substitute when your prescriber has ruled it out, or when the substitute isn't suitable for you, for example because of a past allergic reaction

In those last cases, the pharmacist refers you back to your prescriber. The TGA lists any SSSIs in place on its website, with their dates.

### Contact your doctor about an alternative

If your medicine can't be sourced or substituted, a pharmacist can contact your doctor about an alternative, or suggest you see them. The TGA lists prescribing a similar medicine, or recommending a new treatment that suits you, as other ways your health professionals can help.

## How can you avoid running out?

- **Don't wait for your last dose.** The TGA says early awareness of a shortage can help you, your doctor and your pharmacist manage its impact.
- **Check your repeats.** If you're on your last repeat, book in with your doctor for a new script before you run out.
- **Keep an Active Script List.** An [Active Script List](/how-to-use-escripts/) holds your eScripts in one place. healthdirect explains you can give any pharmacy in the program consent to see it, which makes it easy to fill a script elsewhere. We can register you in store.
- **Only buy what you need.** The TGA says this helps avoid local supply disruptions.
- **Never stop or swap a medicine on your own.** healthdirect says never to stop taking your medicine without talking to your doctor. Talk to a doctor or pharmacist before changing any medicine, too.
- **Never share medicines.** Borrowing someone else's medicine isn't a safe fix. The Better Health Channel advises never giving your medicines to other people.

## What happens when you bring your script to us?

We supply the full quantity on your script, not part of it. If we're short of stock, we tell you when you hand your script in. Then it's your choice: we can order it in for you, or you can take your script elsewhere.

eScripts make that choice easy. healthdirect explains you can take your eScript to any pharmacy set up to dispense them. If you have paper repeats on file somewhere, our guide to [moving your prescriptions](/transfer-prescriptions-new-pharmacy/) explains what to do.

To save time, [send your eScript ahead](/prescriptions/) by email or through the app. You can also [contact us](/contact-us/) to ask about a medicine before you come in.

## How our pharmacists can help

You don't need an appointment to ask. Our pharmacists can:

- explain whether your medicine is affected by a national shortage or a local supply problem
- source most medicines through the several wholesalers we work with
- explain a new brand, strength or substitute, and how to take it
- register you for an Active Script List

## When to see a pharmacist or GP

Talk to a pharmacist or GP if:

- you're down to your last few doses of a medicine you take every day
- you've been told your medicine is unavailable and you're not sure what to do next
- you've changed brand, strength or medicine and feel different, or notice new side effects
- you're not sure how to take a substitute

[Help Medical](/help-medical/), an independent general practice located inside our store, is one option if you need a new script.

If someone has taken too much of a medicine, or the wrong one, call the Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26.

**Call 000 in an emergency.** If someone has trouble breathing, swelling of the tongue or throat, or collapses, call triple zero (000) straight away.
