You don't need any vaccines to enter India from Australia, but several are recommended. Make sure you're up to date with measles (MMR) and tetanus, and have the hepatitis A and typhoid vaccines, as both diseases spread through food and water. Rabies, Japanese encephalitis (JE) and hepatitis B vaccines depend on how long you're going and what you'll be doing. Visiting family? Your risks are often higher, so check your vaccines before every trip. Try to start 6 to 8 weeks before you fly.

Our pharmacist immunisers at Priceline Pharmacy Pacific Fair give most of these vaccines, for anyone aged 5 and over. Walk in or book a time, and see our [travel health](/travel-health/) page for how it works.

## Which vaccines do I need for India?

It depends on your health, your itinerary and who you're staying with, so treat this as a starting point for a chat with a pharmacist or doctor.

| Vaccine | Who should consider it for India | Can we give it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) | Anyone born in or after 1966 without 2 documented doses. Measles is common in India | Yes, and it's free in Queensland if you were born in or after 1966 and haven't had 2 doses |
| Tetanus, diphtheria and whooping cough | Anyone whose last tetanus booster was more than 10 years ago | Yes |
| Hepatitis A | Most travellers | Yes |
| Typhoid | Most travellers, even on short trips, and especially if you're visiting family | Yes |
| Flu | Worth considering for all travellers. Smartraveller says swine flu is widespread in India in winter | Yes |
| COVID-19 | Check whether you're due | Yes |
| Hepatitis B | Longer or frequent trips, visiting family, or if you might need medical or dental treatment | Yes |
| Japanese encephalitis | Stays of a month or more, or shorter trips to rural or farming areas, especially around the monsoon | Yes |
| Rabies (before you travel) | Stays of more than a month, children, people staying with family, and anyone likely to be around dogs or far from medical care | No. We'll point you to a GP or travel clinic |
| Cholera | Not needed by most travellers. Worth discussing for aid workers and some people with medical conditions | Yes |
| Polio | Not usually needed. India only asks for proof from travellers coming from a few countries where polio still spreads, and Australia isn't one of them | Yes |
| Dengue | Some people who've had dengue before | No. We'll point you to a GP or travel clinic |
| Yellow fever | Not needed when you travel from Australia. India only asks for a certificate if you've been in a yellow fever risk country in the 6 days before you arrive | No. We'll point you to a GP or travel clinic |

Most travel vaccines aren't free, so a private fee usually applies. You'll see current prices when you book online. If a vaccine is free for you under the National Immunisation Program or a Queensland Government program, we give it with no fee.

## Visiting family in India? Your risk can be higher

A trip to see family or friends often carries more risk than a typical holiday. The Australian Immunisation Handbook says people visiting friends and relatives are more likely to have close contact with locals, stay in remote or rural areas, and eat or drink higher-risk food and drinks. They're also less likely to get travel health advice or the recommended vaccines before they go.

- **Longer stays.** People visiting family often travel for longer, and some vaccines, such as rabies and JE, are recommended for stays of a month or more.
- **Home cooking and local water.** The Handbook says people visiting friends and relatives appear to be at considerably greater risk of typhoid. It can feel rude to refuse food or drink, so UK travel health advice suggests explaining that you're following your doctor's advice.
- **More time around animals.** Staying in relatives' homes can mean more contact with dogs and other animals, which may raise your risk of rabies.
- **Medical care.** UK advice recommends hepatitis B vaccine for people visiting family and anyone likely to need medical or dental treatment while away. Smartraveller says facilities in remote and rural areas can be very limited, so get travel insurance that covers medical evacuation.
- **Medicines for relatives.** Services Australia says it's illegal to take Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) medicines overseas for anyone other than yourself or someone you're travelling with.
- **Malaria immunity doesn't last.** Any immunity from growing up in a malaria area fades quickly once you live somewhere without malaria, and children who've grown up in Australia have none.

So even if you were born in India or go back every year, check your vaccines before each trip. If you spent your early childhood in India, you may already be immune to hepatitis A, and the Handbook says a blood test before vaccination can help you avoid a dose you don't need.

## Measles: check you're protected

NSW Health lists India among the countries where measles is common. Two doses of MMR vaccine give lifelong protection against measles to 99 in 100 people. If you were born in or after 1966 and don't have records of 2 doses, you can have it free in Queensland. NSW Health advises having the final dose at least 2 weeks before you leave.

After you get home, watch for measles symptoms for 3 weeks: fever, cough, runny nose, sore red eyes and a spotty rash that starts on the head and neck. If they appear, call your GP or the emergency department before you go in, and mention your trip.

## Hepatitis A and typhoid

Both spread through contaminated food and water, and UK travel health advice recommends both vaccines for most travellers to India.

- **Hepatitis A.** One dose gives protection. A second dose 6 to 12 months later gives longer-lasting protection, which is handy if you visit India often.
- **Typhoid.** The Handbook says the risk is considerably greater in the Indian subcontinent than in other regions. The vaccine is a single injection, or capsules you take over several days (from age 6). The injection is estimated to be about 65% effective over 3 years, so you still need to be careful with food and water.

Typhoid is also getting harder to treat. The Handbook says strains resistant to many antibiotics are turning up more often, particularly in the Indian subcontinent, and US travel health advice recommends the typhoid vaccine for India even for short trips.

## Is the rabies vaccine worth it for India?

US travel health advice says India has the highest burden of rabies in the world, and rabid dogs are common. The WHO estimates India accounts for about 35% of the world's rabies deaths. According to healthdirect, rabies is almost always fatal once symptoms appear.

The Handbook recommends deciding on rabies vaccination before you travel after a risk assessment: how likely you are to be around animals, and how easily you could reach emergency medical care. UK travel health advice recommends it for India if you're staying more than a month, going where treatment after a bite is hard to get, or planning activities such as running or cycling. The Handbook also suggests parents consider it for younger children.

Having the vaccine before you go doesn't replace treatment after a bite, but it makes that treatment simpler. You'd usually need just 2 more doses, and no rabies antibody injection, which US travel health advice says is generally unavailable in India.

The course is either 2 visits a week apart or 3 doses over about 3 to 4 weeks. We don't give the rabies vaccine at Pacific Fair. If it sounds right for your trip, see a GP or travel clinic early.

### What to do if an animal bites or scratches you

1. Wash the wound with soap and water for 15 minutes straight away.
2. Apply an antiseptic such as povidone-iodine, and don't cover the wound.
3. Get medical help straight away, even if you've had the rabies vaccine.
4. Keep a record of every dose you're given, including the date, product name and batch number. A photo of the packaging helps.
5. See your GP when you get home to check your course and finish it if needed.

### A warning about fake rabies vaccine

In December 2025, NSW Health warned that a counterfeit (fake) rabies vaccine had been circulating in India since 1 November 2023. If you've had a rabies vaccine in India since then, see your GP and bring any records you have. Doses of the affected product, or of an unknown brand, should be replaced with a rabies vaccine registered in Australia.

## Japanese encephalitis

JE is a virus spread by mosquitoes, mainly in rural and farming areas, such as near rice fields and pig farms. UK travel health advice says it occurs across India, with risk all year that peaks during and just after the monsoon, especially in the north. Most people who catch it have no symptoms, but healthdirect says it can cause severe complications or death.

The Handbook recommends the vaccine for people spending a month or more in areas with JE during its transmission season, including city-based travellers likely to visit rural or farming areas. It's also worth considering for shorter trips in the wet season, with lots of time outdoors, or staying somewhere without air-conditioning, screens or bed nets. The risk is probably negligible on short city trips.

Depending on the type, the JE vaccine is a single dose or a 2-dose course, and we can give it at Pacific Fair.

## Do I need malaria tablets for India?

It depends on where you're going. Smartraveller says malaria is a risk in most parts of India, including major cities. UK travel health advice rates the risk as higher in the states of Mizoram, Odisha and Tripura, where malaria tablets are recommended, and low in the rest of India, including Goa. In low-risk areas, tablets may still be considered for some people, such as long-stay travellers visiting family, people over 70, pregnant women and young children.

Malaria mosquitoes bite mostly between dusk and dawn. See a GP or travel doctor about whether you need malaria tablets, which need a prescription. [Help Medical](/help-medical/), an independent general practice located inside our store, can help.

## Dengue and mosquito bites

Smartraveller says dengue is widespread in India after the monsoon season, and US travel health advice says it peaks in the wet summer months, including the monsoon from June to September. Chikungunya and Zika also occur in India. There's no vaccine for Zika, so if you're pregnant or planning a pregnancy, talk to your GP before you go.

A dengue vaccine is registered in Australia for people aged 4 and over, given as 2 doses 3 months apart. UK travel health advice says it can be considered for people who've had dengue before. We don't give it, so ask a GP or travel doctor whether it suits you.

UK travel health advice says to take particular care to avoid bites during the day. To avoid bites:

- Use an insect repellent containing DEET or picaridin, and reapply it as directed.
- Wear light-coloured, long-sleeved shirts and long pants.
- Sleep in a screened or air-conditioned room, or under a bed net.

Always read the label and follow the directions for use.

## Air pollution and heat

Smartraveller warns that air pollution in parts of India can spike to hazardous levels in winter, from October to February. Children, older people and people with health conditions are more vulnerable, so check the local air quality each day and avoid outdoor activity when it's poor. If you have asthma or a heart or lung condition, see your GP before you go.

Heat-related illness is also a risk, especially in summer. Eat and drink regularly, wear loose, lightweight clothing, and take it easy when it's hottest.

## How to avoid Delhi belly

US travel health advice puts the chance of travellers' diarrhoea in India at more than 6 in 10 over a 2-week trip, and Smartraveller says tap water isn't safe to drink.

- Drink bottled water from a sealed bottle, and use it to brush your teeth.
- Skip ice, as it may be made from tap water.
- Eat food that's freshly cooked and served hot, not food that's been sitting out.
- Peel fruit yourself, and avoid salads and raw vegetables.
- Wash your hands often, and use hand sanitiser before you eat.

If you get diarrhoea, drink plenty of fluids. healthdirect says oral rehydration solution is better than plain water for children and older adults. Get medical help if you have a fever, blood in your poo, signs of dehydration or diarrhoea that won't settle.

Pack a small travel health kit with oral rehydration solution, pain relief, antiseptic, insect repellent and hand sanitiser. See our [travel health checklist](/travel-health-checklist/) for a full countdown to your trip.

## How far ahead should I start?

Ideally 6 to 8 weeks before you fly, as some vaccines need more than one dose or take a couple of weeks to work. For rabies vaccine, healthdirect suggests seeing your doctor 6 to 12 weeks before you go, and Queensland Health says a TB vaccine for a young child should be given at least 3 months before travel. Leaving sooner? Come in anyway. A single dose of hepatitis A vaccine still gives protection, and some courses can be sped up.

## When to see a pharmacist or GP

Talk to your pharmacist about which vaccines suit your trip, what to pack, and how to take your regular medicines with you.

See a GP or travel doctor before you go if you're pregnant or planning a pregnancy, have a weakened immune system or a long-term health condition, are travelling with children under 5, or need rabies vaccine, dengue vaccine or malaria tablets.

After you get home, see a doctor if you have a fever, a rash, diarrhoea that doesn't settle, or any animal bite or scratch, and tell them you've been to India. UK travel health advice says malaria symptoms can appear up to a year after you get home. In an emergency, call 000.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need vaccinations to travel to India?

None are required to enter India when you travel from Australia. But most travellers should be up to date with measles and tetanus, and have the hepatitis A and typhoid vaccines. Hepatitis B, JE and rabies vaccines depend on your plans.

### I grew up in India. Do I still need travel vaccines?

Usually, yes. People visiting friends and relatives often face higher risks than tourists, and any immunity to malaria fades once you live somewhere without it. If you spent your early childhood in India, you may already be immune to hepatitis A, and a blood test can check.

### Do I need a yellow fever certificate for India?

Not if you're travelling from Australia, including via common stopovers in Asia or the Middle East. India asks for one from travellers aged 9 months and over who arrive within 6 days of leaving a country with yellow fever risk, such as some countries in Africa and South America, including a stopover where you left the airport. Without one, you can be held in quarantine for up to 6 days.

### Can I get India travel vaccines at a pharmacy on the Gold Coast?

Yes. At Pacific Fair we give hepatitis A and B, typhoid, cholera, Japanese encephalitis, polio, measles, tetanus and whooping cough, flu and COVID-19 vaccines to people aged 5 and over. For rabies, dengue and yellow fever vaccines, see a GP or travel clinic.

### Do children need vaccines for India?

Children need the same thinking as adults, plus a few extra checks. Babies aged 6 to 11 months can have an extra, early dose of MMR, which Queensland funds. Children under 5 may need a tuberculosis (TB) vaccine, as India has high TB rates. The Handbook also suggests parents consider rabies vaccination for younger children. We vaccinate from age 5, so see a GP for younger children.
