Hives (urticaria) are itchy, raised welts on the skin that come and go, often moving from one place to another within hours. They're common: the Australasian Society of Clinical Immunology and Allergy (ASCIA) says up to one in five people get hives at some point. In children, a viral infection is a very common trigger, and often no cause is found. A non-drowsy antihistamine from the pharmacy usually helps. **Call 000** straight away if hives come with swelling of the tongue or throat, or trouble breathing.

## What do hives look like?

- raised, itchy welts, which can be small spots or large patches that join together
- pink or red on lighter skin, and harder to see on darker skin
- welts that fade in one place and appear in another
- sometimes swelling of the lips, eyelids, hands or feet

## What causes hives?

ASCIA lists a range of triggers:

- **Infections,** especially viral infections in children.
- **Allergic reactions,** for example to a food, a medicine or an insect sting. These usually come on quickly after contact.
- **Physical triggers** in some people, such as heat, cold, sunlight, rubbing or scratching, and pressure from tight clothing or carrying heavy bags.

Often no cause is found. ASCIA notes that hives lasting more than a few days usually aren't caused by an allergy.

## Acute and chronic hives

- **Acute hives** last from a few days to a few weeks.
- **Chronic hives** appear on most days for more than six weeks. They usually settle in the end, but it can take months or even years, so see your GP for a plan.

## How can I treat hives?

- **Take a non-drowsy antihistamine.** ASCIA says these are the main treatment. A pharmacist can help you choose one that suits your age and any other medicines you take. Some antihistamines cause drowsiness and aren't suitable for young children.
- **Avoid things that make it worse,** such as getting hot, spicy food and alcohol.
- **Wear loose, cool clothing,** and avoid tight waistbands and straps.
- **Cool the itch** with a cool shower or a cool, damp cloth.

Always read the label and follow the directions for use.

## When is it an emergency?

ASCIA says hives with swelling of the tongue or throat, difficulty breathing, or signs of low blood pressure, such as dizziness or collapse, could be anaphylaxis. Anaphylaxis is a medical emergency:

1. Lay the person flat, or let them sit with their legs out in front of them if breathing is hard.
2. Give their adrenaline injector if they have one.
3. Call 000.

Our guide to [bites and stings](/bites-and-stings-first-aid/) lists the signs of anaphylaxis and the first aid steps in full.

## When should I see a doctor?

- **Straight away** if hives come with a high fever, bruising or bleeding into the skin, purple lumps that last several days, or sore joints.
- If hives last more than six weeks, or keep coming back.
- If you think a food, medicine or sting caused them, so your GP can arrange allergy testing if it's needed.
- If a baby or young child with hives seems unwell.
