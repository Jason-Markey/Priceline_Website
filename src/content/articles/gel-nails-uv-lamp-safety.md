Gel nails can be part of your routine, but they come with two risks worth understanding: the UV your hands get from the curing lamp, and allergy to the acrylate chemicals that gel products are built on. Both are easy to reduce with a little prep, careful application and patient removal.

Here's what our beauty team and pharmacists at Pacific Fair want you to know before your next set, whether you book a salon or use a kit at home.

## How do gel nails work?

Gel polish stays soft until it's cured (hardened) under a nail lamp. Salons use professional UV or LED lamps, and many at-home kits come with their own UV lamp. Once it's cured, gel needs an acetone remover to soften it before it comes off. You can see the kits, lamps and removers in the [gel nail polish range on priceline.com.au](https://www.priceline.com.au/c/gel-nail-polish-kits).

Gel products, like acrylic nails and many nail glues, are based on acrylates. DermNet's list of nail cosmetic allergens includes 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA).

## Are UV nail lamps safe?

A nail lamp isn't a solarium: each session is short and only your hands are under the light. But it's still UV, and UV exposure adds up over a lifetime. DermNet explains that UVA penetrates the lower layers of the skin and induces premature ageing, and that it suppresses the skin's immune system, which may play a role in the development of skin cancers. More generally, DermNet notes that the greater your exposure to UV radiation, the greater the likelihood of developing skin cancer.

The backs of your hands already get plenty of sun on the Gold Coast, on the steering wheel, at the beach and on the school run, so there's no reason to add UV you don't need. A little prep keeps the extra dose down:

- **Sunscreen first.** Apply an SPF50 or SPF50+ broad-spectrum sunscreen to the backs of your hands and fingers before your appointment. Cancer Council's general advice is to apply sunscreen to clean, dry skin 20 minutes before you go outside, and the same timing works here. Ask your nail technician whether they'd like you to keep it off the nails themselves.
- **Or cover up.** Fingerless gloves that cover the backs of your hands leave only your fingertips exposed.
- **Don't over-cure.** Whatever type of lamp is used, follow the curing time for that product rather than leaving your hands under the light for longer.
- **Look after your hands every day.** Sunscreen is one part of sun protection; our [Gold Coast sun safety guide](/sun-safety-gold-coast/) covers the rest.

## Can you be allergic to gel nails?

Yes. DermNet explains that acrylates in their liquid (monomer) form are very strong irritants and allergens. Once they're fully cured into a hard polymer they're generally inert, so the risk comes mainly from uncured product touching your skin. DermNet also notes that allergic reactions to nail polish are most commonly due to wet product, although some people react to dried polish too.

### Signs of an acrylate allergy

According to DermNet, watch for:

- itching, burning, redness and swelling of the fingertips and hands
- dermatitis on the eyelids, face, around the mouth and chin, in and behind the ears or on the sides of the neck, often spread there by your hands
- the nail lifting off the nail bed (onycholysis), or infection around the nail (paronychia)
- tingling or prickling in the fingertips, or nail loss, which DermNet describes as rare but very serious reactions

### Why an acrylate allergy matters beyond your nails

Once you're allergic, DermNet's advice is to avoid all products containing the allergen. DermNet lists acrylates in dental materials, orthopaedic bone cements and medical spray adhesives as well as artificial nails, and advises telling your doctor, pharmacist, dentist and beautician about the allergy. That's a good reason to take early symptoms seriously.

### Are DIY gel kits riskier?

They can be. At home, it's easier for uncured gel to end up on your cuticles and the skin around your nails without you noticing. If the gel isn't fully cured, more of the reactive liquid form is left behind. Both mean more skin contact with the form of acrylate that DermNet says to avoid.

If you do your own nails:

- keep gel off your skin and cuticles, and wipe away any spill before you cure
- use the lamp made for or recommended with your gel, and follow the curing times on the pack
- try not to touch uncured gel, and wash your hands when you're done
- stop and get advice if your fingertips, eyelids or face itch or swell after a manicure

## How do you remove gel nails without damage?

Don't peel or pick gel off, however tempting it is when an edge lifts. That's repeated trauma to your nails, and DermNet lists repetitive trauma, including manicures, among the causes of onycholysis. Instead:

1. Gently buff the shiny top layer so the remover can get in.
2. Soak with an acetone remover, following the directions on the pack.
3. Ease off the softened gel with an orangewood stick. If it won't budge, soak again rather than scraping.
4. Wash your hands, then moisturise your nails and cuticles. DermNet notes that excessive use of remover can leave nails dry and brittle.

If you'd rather not, ask the salon that applied your gel to remove it.

## How do you keep your natural nails healthy?

- Keep nails short and avoid knocks and trauma, which DermNet advises for lifting nails.
- Wear gloves for wet work like washing up. DermNet suggests light cotton gloves under vinyl gloves.
- Long-term use of coloured enamels can discolour the nail plate, so the occasional break between sets lets you see how your natural nails are doing.
- If a nail starts lifting, keep it trimmed short and skip polish and remover until it grows out.

### Signs of a nail infection

Paronychia is inflammation of the skin around a nail. DermNet lists manicuring and the application of sculptured or artificial nails among the triggers. Watch for pain, redness and swelling of the nail fold, sometimes with yellow pus under the cuticle. Acute paronychia usually develops over a few hours. A yellow, thickened or crumbly nail is more likely to be a fungal infection: see our guide to [nail fungus treatment](/nail-fungus-treatment/).

## When to see a pharmacist or GP

See a pharmacist or GP (our in-store [Help Medical GPs](/help-medical/) are one option) if you have:

- redness, swelling and pain around a nail, especially with pus, as some infections need prescription treatment or drainage
- an itchy or swollen rash on your fingertips, eyelids or face after a manicure, which may need patch testing to confirm an allergy
- tingling or prickling in your fingertips after gel or acrylic nails
- a nail that is lifting away from the nail bed, changing colour or thickening
- redness spreading up your finger or hand, or a fever
- any new or changing spot on your hands
