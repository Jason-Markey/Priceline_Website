When you start a new medicine, ask what it's for, how to take it and for how long. Ask what to do if you miss a dose, and which side effects to watch for. Then check it's safe with your other medicines, with alcohol and for driving.

healthdirect reminds you that you have the right to ask your pharmacist or doctor about the medicines you're prescribed. Here's a checklist to bring along, why each question matters, and where to find the answers later.

## What should I ask about a new medicine?

You don't need to ask everything at once. Pick the questions that matter most to you, or bring this list and work through it with your pharmacist.

### What is it for?

Knowing why you're taking a medicine helps you take it properly and notice whether it's helping. healthdirect also suggests asking about the likely benefits, and what happens if you don't take it.

### How and when do I take it?

Ask how much to take, how often, and at what times of day. The Better Health Channel also suggests asking whether to take it on a full or empty stomach, and whether tablets can be crushed or chewed.

Why it matters: food can change how some medicines work. healthdirect gives "some medicines you need to take with food" as an example of what to note on your medicines list.

### How long do I take it for?

Some medicines are a short course, like antibiotics, while others are long term. healthdirect advises checking with your doctor before stopping a medicine earlier than prescribed. Some medicines need to be stopped slowly to reduce the chance of withdrawal effects.

### What if I miss a dose?

The answer is different for each medicine, so ask before it happens. healthdirect notes that the Consumer Medicine Information (CMI) leaflet also explains what to do if you miss a dose.

### What side effects should I look out for?

healthdirect suggests asking about both common side effects, which are usually less serious, and serious ones, so you know what to do if they happen. The Better Health Channel adds a useful follow-up: what are the signs of a serious reaction I need to watch out for?

### Will it interact with my other medicines?

Tell your pharmacist about everything you take, including over-the-counter medicines, vitamins and herbal products.

Why it matters: the Better Health Channel warns that mixing medicines can cause side effects. Our guide to [supplements that can interact with your medicines](/supplements-that-interact-with-medicines/) covers common examples. Always read the label and follow the directions for use.

### Can I drink alcohol with it?

healthdirect warns that alcohol can interact with many medicines, even in small amounts. It can change how both the alcohol and the medicine affect you, and affect your ability to drive, work safely or care for children. Our guide to [alcohol and antibiotics](/alcohol-and-antibiotics/) explains more.

### Is it safe to drive?

The Queensland Government says some medicines can affect your driving, including some over-the-counter cold and flu tablets. Ask whether your new medicine could make you drowsy or dizzy. Watch for any effects when you start a medicine or change a dose.

### How should I store it?

The Better Health Channel advises keeping medicines in their original containers, in a cool, dry place out of direct sunlight. Some medicines need to be refrigerated, so check the label. In a humid Gold Coast summer, a steamy bathroom isn't a cool, dry place.

### How will I know it's working?

Some medicines work almost straight away. healthdirect explains that others, such as antidepressants, may take weeks before you notice a difference. Ask what to expect, and what to do if you don't feel it's working.

### Is there a generic?

A generic is another brand of the same medicine. Generics must meet the same standards for quality, safety and effectiveness as the original brand, though they may look different. Choosing a generic or the brand is your choice, unless your doctor has marked the script "brand substitution not permitted". Read more in [generic vs brand-name medicines](/generic-vs-brand-medicines/).

## What are the Choosing Wisely questions?

Choosing Wisely Australia suggests five questions to ask your doctor or other health care provider before any test, treatment or procedure. Applied to a new medicine, they are:

1. Do I really need it?
2. What are the risks?
3. Are there simpler, safer options?
4. What happens if I don't do anything?
5. What are the costs?

Choosing Wisely notes that costs can be financial, emotional or a cost of your time.

## What is a Consumer Medicine Information leaflet?

A CMI is a leaflet about the safe and effective use of a medicine. The TGA explains that it's written by the pharmaceutical company responsible for the medicine. healthdirect says all prescription medicines, and some non-prescription medicines, have one.

According to healthdirect, a CMI covers:

- the active and other ingredients
- how to store the medicine
- what to do if you miss a dose
- possible interactions with other medicines
- what to do with leftover medicine

A CMI is often inside the pack, and your pharmacist or doctor can print one for you. You can also search for CMIs on the TGA website. healthdirect suggests reading it before you start, checking that the brand name matches your medicine, and keeping your CMIs together.

## Why keep a current medicines list?

healthdirect says medicine lists help make sure everyone involved in your healthcare knows what you are taking. The Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care suggests using your list to tell your doctor and pharmacist what you take. Include vitamins and supplements too.

For each medicine, healthdirect suggests noting:

- what it's for
- the dose
- when to take it
- how to take it, for example with food

Keep it with you. The Better Health Channel suggests carrying your list in case of an emergency or a hospital stay. If you help an older relative, see our guide to [helping a parent manage their medicines](/help-elderly-parent-with-medication/).

## Taking several medicines? Ask about a review

Starting something new is a good time to look at everything you take. healthdirect says taking 5 or more medicines is called polypharmacy. It suggests having your medicines checked every 6 to 12 months. You can also ask for a review any time, such as when you start or stop a medicine.

Our pharmacists offer [medication reviews](/medication-reviews/), and [Webster packs](/medication-packing/) can help if you take a lot of medicines. Your GP can also refer you for a Home Medicines Review, where a pharmacist visits you at home.

If you finish a course or a medicine is stopped, ask what to do with any leftovers. Here's [how to dispose of old medicines](/how-to-dispose-of-old-medicines/) for free.

## When to see a pharmacist or GP

Talk to your pharmacist or GP if:

- you notice a side effect, or feel worse, after starting a new medicine
- you don't feel your medicine is working
- you've missed doses and aren't sure what to do
- you want to stop a medicine before the course is finished

[Help Medical](/help-medical/), an independent general practice located inside our store, is one option if you need a doctor.

If someone has made a mistake with a medicine or taken too much, call the Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26.

**Call 000 in an emergency.** Signs of a severe allergic reaction include difficult or noisy breathing, swelling of the tongue or throat, a wheeze, or persistent dizziness or collapse. Call triple zero (000) immediately.
