For a day outdoors on the Gold Coast, choose SPF50 or SPF50+. That's what Cancer Council recommends, and SPF50+ is the highest rating allowed on Australian sunscreens. But the gap between SPF30 and SPF50+ is smaller than the numbers suggest: in lab testing, SPF30 filters about 96.7% of UVB rays and SPF50+ about 98.3%. How much sunscreen you put on, and how often you reapply it, matters more than the number on the bottle.

That's worth knowing here. Cancer Council Queensland says that every day in Queensland the UV Index reaches 3 or above, the point where UV is too strong to be outside without protecting your skin. Whichever SPF you buy, you'll be using a lot of it, so here's what the numbers mean and how to get the protection the label promises.

## How much more protection does SPF50+ give?

SPF stands for sun protection factor. It tells you how well a sunscreen filters UVB, the type of UV radiation mainly responsible for sunburn. SunSmart explains that the rating comes from lab tests in which sunscreen is applied liberally and compared with bare skin. A simple way to read it: SPF30 lets roughly one-thirtieth of UVB through to your skin, and SPF50 about one-fiftieth.

Here's how SunSmart, Cancer Council Victoria's sun protection program, compares the three ratings you'll see on Australian shelves:

| Label | UVB filtered | UVB reaching your skin | Protection level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SPF30 | about 96.7% | about 3.3% (1 in 30) | High |
| SPF50 | about 98% | about 2% (1 in 50) | High |
| SPF50+ | about 98.3% | about 1.7% (1 in 60) | Very high |

The protection level column uses Cancer Council's terms: SPF30 to SPF50 give a high level of protection, and SPF50+ gives very high protection.

Looked at one way, the step up is small. The TGA says the difference in protection between SPF30 and SPF50 is minimal, and SunSmart says it's safe to keep using an SPF30 you already own, as it still gives a high level of protection. Looked at another way, SPF50+ lets through about half as much UVB as SPF30 (1.7% compared with 3.3%). For time outdoors, Cancer Council recommends SPF50 or SPF50+.

## What does SPF50+ mean on an Australian label?

Sunscreens in Australia are regulated by the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA), and SPF50+ is the highest SPF label currently allowed. Cancer Council explains that the plus sign means "more than". According to the TGA, under the current Australian standard a product must have a test result of at least SPF60 to be labelled SPF50+. That's why you won't see SPF70 or SPF100 on an Australian label.

SPF only describes UVB, so check the label also says broad spectrum (it filters UVA too) and water resistant, for the time stated. Our [sunscreen buyer's checklist](/best-sunscreen-gold-coast/) explains each label term.

## Why does the amount you apply matter more than the number?

The TGA notes that most people don't use enough sunscreen. Because the SPF rating is based on a generous layer, a thin smear won't give you the protection shown on the label, whatever the number. Put the TGA's two points together (a minimal difference between SPF30 and SPF50, and most people applying too little) and it's clear the habits below matter more than choosing between 30 and 50.

Cancer Council's guide for an adult is about 7 teaspoons (around 35 mL) for a whole-body application, put on 20 minutes before you go outside and reapplied every two hours, and after swimming, sweating or towelling.

A few habits that make this easier on the coast:

- Put sunscreen on at home, before you get to the beach, when it's easy to do properly.
- Set a phone reminder for the two-hour top-up.
- Keep a bottle in your bag, but out of the hot car.
- Don't forget your ears, the back of your neck, the tops of your feet and along your hairline.

## Is SPF30 enough for every day?

For mostly indoor days with only brief sun exposure, Cancer Council says an SPF30 product provides adequate protection. Save the full SPF50+ application, hat and shade for time outdoors.

You do need some protection every day here: Cancer Council Queensland says the UV Index reaches 3 or above every day in Queensland, even on cloudy and cool days. Our guide to [sun safety on the Gold Coast](/sun-safety-gold-coast/) covers daily habits and all five sun safety steps.

## When to see a pharmacist or GP

Talk to a pharmacist if:

- a sunscreen stings, itches or causes a rash (stop using it and bring it in so we can help you find an alternative)
- you're choosing sun protection for a baby, a young child or someone with very sensitive skin

See a GP ([Help Medical](/help-medical/), an independent general practice located inside our store, is one option) if:

- your sunburn has large areas of blistering, severe pain, or signs of infection such as fever or yellow crusts
- you have signs of dehydration after time in the sun, such as dizziness, tiredness or passing less urine
- you notice a spot or mole that is new, changing or looks different from your others

**Call 000 in an emergency.** healthdirect says heatstroke is a medical emergency: if someone becomes confused, has a seizure or loses consciousness after time in the sun, call triple zero (000) and ask for an ambulance.
