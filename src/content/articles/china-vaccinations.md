You don't need any vaccines to enter China from Australia, but a few are well worth having. Make sure you're up to date with measles (MMR), tetanus and flu, and talk to a pharmacist about hepatitis A and typhoid, which spread through contaminated food and water. Hepatitis B, Japanese encephalitis (JE) and rabies vaccines depend on how long you're staying and where you're going. Visiting family, long stays and trips to the countryside can all carry more risk. There's no malaria in China. Try to start 6 to 8 weeks before you fly.

Our pharmacist immunisers at Priceline Pharmacy Pacific Fair give most of these vaccines, for anyone aged 5 and over. Walk in or book a time, and see our [travel health](/travel-health/) page for how it works.

## Which vaccines do I need for China?

China is Australians' fourth most-visited overseas country, with about 744,000 short-term trips in 2025-26, according to the Australian Bureau of Statistics. The right vaccines depend on your health, how long you're staying and what you're planning, so treat this as a starting point for a chat with a pharmacist or doctor. This guide covers mainland China. Hong Kong and Macau have separate travel advice.

| Vaccine | Who should consider it for China | Can we give it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) | Anyone born in or after 1966 without 2 documented doses | Yes, and it's free in Queensland if you were born in or after 1966 and haven't had 2 doses |
| Tetanus, diphtheria and whooping cough | Anyone whose last tetanus booster was more than 10 years ago | Yes |
| Flu | Worth considering for all travellers, especially from October to May | Yes |
| Hepatitis A | Recommended for all travellers who haven't had it before | Yes |
| Typhoid | Most travellers, especially people visiting family or rural areas | Yes |
| Hepatitis B | Worth considering if you're not already immune, and recommended for long or frequent trips | Yes |
| Japanese encephalitis | Stays of a month or more, or shorter trips with time in rural and farming areas. Not usually needed for short city trips | Yes |
| Rabies (before you travel) | Long stays, rural trips, children, and anyone likely to be around dogs or other animals | No. We'll point you to a GP or travel clinic |
| Dengue | Mainly people who've had dengue before and are heading to the southern provinces | No. We'll point you to a GP or travel clinic |
| Yellow fever | Not needed when you fly from Australia | No. We'll point you to a GP or travel clinic |

Most travel vaccines aren't free, so a private fee usually applies. You'll see current prices when you book online. If a vaccine is free for you, like MMR for eligible adults, we give it with no fee.

## Visiting family? Your risks are different

If you're going to see family or friends, you may be more likely to get sick than a tourist. The Australian Immunisation Handbook says people visiting friends and relatives are more likely to have close contact with local people, stay in rural areas and have higher-risk food and drinks, and less likely to get health advice before they go. UK travel health advice adds that these trips tend to be longer, and staying in relatives' homes can mean more contact with animals.

- **Typhoid** vaccine is especially recommended for family visits.
- **Staying a month or more, or spending time in the countryside?** Ask about JE and rabies vaccines.
- **Family meals.** It can feel rude to say no. Hot, freshly cooked dishes and tea made with boiled water are usually the safer choices.
- **Taking medicines for relatives?** It's illegal to take or send PBS medicines overseas for anyone other than you or someone travelling with you, even family.

## Measles, tetanus and flu

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) says high vaccination rates have kept measles low in China since 2020. Still, check your MMR before any overseas trip: the Handbook says under-vaccinated young adult travellers are responsible for most current measles outbreaks in Australia. Two doses of MMR vaccine give lifelong protection against measles to 99 in 100 people. If you were born in or after 1966 and don't have records of 2 doses, you can have it free in Queensland. NSW Health advises having your final dose at least 2 weeks before you leave.

**Tetanus.** Have a booster if your last dose was more than 10 years ago. We give it combined with diphtheria and whooping cough.

**Flu.** The Handbook says flu is the most common vaccine-preventable infection in travellers. The Northern Hemisphere flu season usually runs from October to May, so a Christmas trip, or a visit for Lunar New Year (6 February 2027), falls right in it. If you've already had this year's flu vaccine, the Handbook says you may have a second dose before you go.

## Hepatitis A, typhoid and hepatitis B

- **Hepatitis A** spreads through contaminated food and water. UK travel health advice recommends the vaccine for all travellers to China who haven't had it. One dose gives protection, and a second dose 6 to 12 months later makes it last longer. If you spent your early childhood somewhere hepatitis A is common, you may already be immune. A blood test can check, but the vaccine does no harm if you are.
- **Typhoid** also spreads through food and water. The CDC says it isn't a significant risk in China's major cities, but suggests people visiting rural areas or family strongly consider the vaccine. It's a single injection, or capsules taken over several days from age 6. The injection is estimated to be about 65% effective over 3 years, and there's no vaccine for paratyphoid, a similar illness that's more common in China. So care with food and water still matters.
- **Hepatitis B** is common across most of East and Southeast Asia, according to the Handbook, which recommends the vaccine for long or frequent trips. The CDC suggests anyone travelling to China who isn't immune consider it. The usual course is 3 doses over 6 months, and a faster schedule is possible if you're short on time.

## Japanese encephalitis

JE is a virus spread by mosquitoes. Most people who catch it have no symptoms, but it can cause permanent brain injury or death. UK travel health advice says JE is found throughout China except Qinghai and Xinjiang, mainly in rural areas, with most cases between May and October.

The Handbook recommends the vaccine for people spending a month or more in areas where JE is found during its season, even if they're based in a city but will visit rural areas. It's worth considering for shorter trips with time in the countryside or outdoors. For a short trip to the big cities only, the risk is very small. Depending on the type, the vaccine is a single dose or a 2-dose course, and we can give it at Pacific Fair.

## Is the rabies vaccine worth it for China?

Rabies is found in animals in China, especially free-roaming dogs, and healthdirect says it almost always causes death once symptoms appear. The CDC advises treating any dog or other mammal bite anywhere in China, including in cities, as a high risk. It also warns that rabies immunoglobulin, an injection you may need after a bite if you haven't been vaccinated, is often unavailable to international standards. That can mean travelling to Bangkok, Hong Kong or home for treatment. Having the vaccine before you go means you won't need it.

The Handbook recommends deciding on rabies vaccination before you travel after a risk assessment: how likely you are to be around animals, and how easily you could reach medical care. The CDC suggests strongly considering it for rural trips and long stays, and the Handbook suggests parents consider it for younger children. The course is either 2 visits a week apart or 3 doses over about 3 to 4 weeks.

We don't give the rabies vaccine at Pacific Fair. If it sounds right for your trip, see a GP or travel clinic early, as the course takes time.

### What to do if an animal bites or scratches you

1. Wash the wound with soap and water for 15 minutes straight away.
2. Apply an antiseptic such as povidone-iodine, and don't cover the wound.
3. Get medical help urgently, the same day if you can, even if you've had the rabies vaccine.
4. Ask for a written record of any treatment you're given, and see your GP when you get home.

## Do I need malaria tablets for China?

No. The World Health Organization certified China malaria-free in 2021, after 4 years in a row with no locally acquired cases. In the 1940s, China had about 30 million cases a year. UK travel health advice says there's no malaria risk in China now. If your trip continues to other countries, check the advice for each one, and see a GP or travel doctor if you need malaria tablets, which need a prescription.

## Dengue and mosquito bites

Dengue is a risk in China, mostly in the south. UK travel health advice lists Fujian, Guangdong, Guangxi, Hainan, Yunnan and Zhejiang among the main affected areas, and the UK Foreign Office says it's mainly present in the rainy season, from May to November. Chikungunya, another virus spread by mosquitoes, has also caused outbreaks in Yunnan, Guangdong and Zhejiang.

A dengue vaccine is registered in Australia for people aged 4 and over, given as 2 doses 3 months apart. UK advice mainly suggests it for people who've had dengue before. We don't offer it, so ask a GP or travel doctor whether it suits you.

To avoid bites, use an insect repellent containing DEET, picaridin or oil of lemon eucalyptus, and wear loose-fitting, light-coloured clothing and covered shoes. Choose rooms with insect screens, or sleep under a mosquito net. Always read the label and follow the directions for use.

## Bird flu: avoid live animal markets

Bird flu rarely spreads to people, but most human cases reported worldwide have been in China, according to the Government of Canada. It can spread through close contact with infected birds, or visits to live bird and animal markets and poultry farms.

- Stay away from live animal markets, poultry farms and places where poultry are slaughtered.
- Don't touch birds, alive or dead, including backyard chickens and ducks.
- Make sure poultry and eggs are thoroughly cooked, and wash your hands often.

Your seasonal flu vaccine won't protect you against bird flu.

## Air pollution and lung conditions

The CDC says air quality has greatly improved in Beijing, but pollution is still a problem in many parts of China. People with heart or lung conditions, children and older adults are the most likely to be affected.

If you have asthma or another heart or lung condition, see your doctor before you go, pack enough of your regular medicines, and check the local air quality before heading outdoors. Surgical masks don't filter the fine particles in air pollution. The CDC says a well-fitted N95 mask might help on very bad days, but UK advice warns masks may make breathing harder if you have a lung condition, so ask your doctor first.

## Going to Tibet or other high places?

Altitude sickness can start above about 2,500 metres, with a headache, cough, racing heart or trouble breathing. According to the CDC, Lhasa in Tibet sits at 3,658 metres, Shangri-La at 3,280 metres, and the railway to Tibet climbs above 5,000 metres.

- Go up slowly. Try not to sleep above about 2,500 metres on your first night, and once you're above 3,000 metres, don't climb more than 500 metres a day.
- Where possible, avoid going from below 1,200 metres to above 3,500 metres in one day, as you would by flying straight into Lhasa from a low-lying city.
- If symptoms start, don't go any higher, and head down if they get worse.

See your doctor before you go if you have heart or lung problems. A doctor can also give you medicine to help you adjust. The CDC says supply in China is unreliable, so take it with you.

## Food and water in China

The CDC says tap water isn't safe to drink in China, even in the big cities.

- Drink sealed bottled water or boiled water, and use it to brush your teeth.
- Skip ice. Hot tea and coffee made with boiled water are usually safe.
- Eat food that's freshly cooked and served piping hot, and avoid raw or undercooked meat and seafood, and unpasteurised milk.
- Peel fruit yourself, and go easy on salads.
- Wash your hands before you eat, or use hand sanitiser.
- Avoid swimming in rivers and lakes. The risk is very low, but fresh water can carry a parasite that causes schistosomiasis.

Pack a small travel health kit with oral rehydration solution, pain relief, antiseptic and insect repellent. See our [travel health checklist](/travel-health-checklist/) for a full countdown to your trip.

## How far ahead should I start?

Ideally 6 to 8 weeks before you fly, as some vaccines need more than one dose or take a couple of weeks to work. Leaving sooner, perhaps for a family emergency? Come in anyway. A single dose of hepatitis A vaccine still gives protection, and some courses can be sped up.

## When to see a pharmacist or GP

Talk to your pharmacist about which vaccines suit your trip, what to pack, and how to take your regular medicines with you. Some medicines may not be available in China, so take enough for the whole trip.

See a GP or travel doctor before you go if you're pregnant, have a weakened immune system or a long-term health condition, are travelling with children under 5, are heading to high altitude, or need rabies vaccine, dengue vaccine or malaria tablets. [Help Medical](/help-medical/), an independent general practice located inside our store, can help.

After you get home, see a doctor if you have a fever, a rash, diarrhoea that doesn't settle, or any animal bite or scratch, and tell them you've been to China. In an emergency, call 000.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need vaccinations to travel to China?

None are required to enter China from Australia. But being up to date with measles, tetanus and flu, and talking to a pharmacist about hepatitis A and typhoid vaccines, is recommended for most travellers.

### Do I need extra vaccines if I'm visiting family in China?

You may. People visiting friends and relatives tend to stay longer, eat local food and stay in homes, which can raise the risk of illnesses such as typhoid and rabies. Typhoid vaccine is especially recommended, and JE and rabies vaccines are worth discussing for longer stays or time in the countryside.

### Is there malaria in China?

No. The World Health Organization certified China malaria-free in 2021, so you don't need malaria tablets for China itself.
