You don't need any vaccines to enter China from Australia, but a few are well worth having. Make sure you're up to date with measles (MMR), tetanus and flu, and talk to a pharmacist about hepatitis A and typhoid, which spread through food and water. Hepatitis B, Japanese encephalitis (JE) and rabies vaccines depend on how long you're staying, where you're going and whether you're visiting family. China is malaria-free. Try to start 6 to 8 weeks before you fly.

Our pharmacist immunisers at Priceline Pharmacy Pacific Fair give most of these vaccines, for anyone aged 5 and over. Walk in or book a time, and see our [travel health](/travel-health/) page for how it works.

## Which vaccines do I need for China?

China is Australians' fourth most-visited overseas country, with about 744,000 short-term trips in 2025-26, according to the Australian Bureau of Statistics. The right mix depends on your health and plans, so treat this table as a starting point for a chat with a pharmacist or doctor. This guide covers mainland China. Hong Kong and Macau have separate Smartraveller pages.

| Vaccine | Who should consider it for China | Can we give it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) | Anyone born in or after 1966 without 2 documented doses | Yes, and it's free in Queensland if you were born in or after 1966 and haven't had 2 doses |
| Tetanus, diphtheria and whooping cough | Anyone whose last tetanus booster was more than 10 years ago | Yes |
| Flu | Worth considering for all travellers, especially from October to May | Yes |
| Hepatitis A | All travellers who haven't had it | Yes |
| Typhoid | Most travellers, especially people visiting family or rural areas | Yes |
| Hepatitis B | Anyone who isn't immune, especially for long or frequent trips | Yes |
| Japanese encephalitis | Stays of a month or more, or shorter trips with time in rural areas. Not usually needed for short city trips | Yes |
| Rabies (before you travel) | Long stays, rural trips, children, and anyone likely to be around dogs | No. We'll point you to a GP or travel clinic |
| Dengue | Mainly people who've had dengue before | No. We'll point you to a GP or travel clinic |
| Yellow fever | Not needed when you fly from Australia. Only required if you arrive from, or transit through, a country with yellow fever risk | No. We'll point you to a GP or travel clinic |

Most travel vaccines aren't free, so a private fee usually applies. You'll see current prices when you book online. If a vaccine is free for you, such as MMR, there's no fee.

## Visiting family? Your risks are different

The Australian Immunisation Handbook says people visiting friends and relatives are more likely to have close contact with local people, stay in rural areas and have higher-risk food and drinks, and less likely to get health advice before they go. UK travel health advice adds that these trips are often longer, and staying in relatives' homes can mean more contact with animals.

- **Typhoid** vaccine is especially recommended for family visits.
- **Long stay, or time in the countryside?** Ask about JE and rabies vaccines.
- **Family meals.** It can feel rude to refuse food, so where you can, choose hot, freshly cooked dishes and tea made with boiled water.
- **Medicines for relatives.** It's illegal to take or send PBS medicines overseas for anyone except yourself or someone travelling with you, even family.

## Measles, tetanus and flu

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) says high vaccination rates have kept measles low in China since 2020, but check your MMR before any overseas trip. Two doses give lifelong protection against measles to 99 in 100 people. If you were born in or after 1966 and don't have records of 2 doses, you can have it free in Queensland. NSW Health advises having your final dose at least 2 weeks before you leave.

Have a tetanus booster if your last one was more than 10 years ago. The Northern Hemisphere flu season usually runs from October to May, so a Christmas visit, or one for Lunar New Year (6 February 2027), falls right in it. If you've already had this year's flu vaccine, the Handbook says you may have a second dose before you go.

## Hepatitis A, typhoid and hepatitis B

- **Hepatitis A.** UK travel health advice recommends it for all travellers to China who haven't had it. One dose gives protection, and a second dose 6 to 12 months later makes it last longer.
- **Typhoid.** The CDC says it isn't a significant risk in China's big cities, but people visiting rural areas or family should strongly consider the vaccine. It's a single injection, or capsules taken over several days from age 6. The injection is about 65% effective over 3 years, so care with food and water still matters.
- **Hepatitis B** is common in most of East and Southeast Asia. The Handbook recommends the vaccine for long or frequent trips, and the CDC suggests anyone going to China who isn't immune consider it. The course is 3 doses over 6 months, and a faster schedule may be possible if you're short on time.

## Japanese encephalitis

JE is a virus spread by mosquitoes. Most people who catch it have no symptoms, but it can cause permanent brain injury or death. UK travel health advice says it occurs across China except Qinghai and Xinjiang, with most cases between May and October.

The Handbook recommends the vaccine if you'll spend a month or more in areas with JE during its season, even if you're based in a city but will visit rural areas. It's worth considering for shorter trips with time in the countryside, but for short city trips the risk is very low. Depending on the type, it's a single dose or a 2-dose course, and we can give it at Pacific Fair.

## Is the rabies vaccine worth it for China?

Rabies is found in animals in China, especially free-roaming dogs, and healthdirect says it almost always causes death once symptoms appear. The CDC advises treating any dog or other mammal bite in China, even in a city, as high risk. It also warns that an injection unvaccinated people may need after a bite is often not available to international standards, so you could need to go to Bangkok, Hong Kong or home for treatment. Having the vaccine before you go means you won't need that injection.

The Handbook recommends deciding before you travel, based on how likely you are to be around animals and how easily you could get medical care, and suggests parents consider it for younger children. The course is 2 visits a week apart or 3 doses over about 3 to 4 weeks. We don't give it at Pacific Fair, so see a GP or travel clinic early.

### What to do if an animal bites or scratches you

1. Wash the wound with soap and water for 15 minutes straight away.
2. Apply an antiseptic such as povidone-iodine, and don't cover the wound.
3. Get medical help urgently, the same day if you can, even if you've had the rabies vaccine.
4. Ask for a written record of any treatment you're given, and see your GP when you get home.

## Do I need malaria tablets for China?

No. The World Health Organization certified China malaria-free in 2021. If your trip takes in other countries, check the advice for each one, and see a GP or travel doctor if you need malaria tablets, which need a prescription.

## Dengue and mosquito bites

Dengue is a risk in China, mostly in the south, in places such as Guangdong, Fujian, Guangxi, Hainan and Yunnan, and mainly in the rainy season from May to November. A dengue vaccine is registered in Australia for people aged 4 and over, but it's mainly for people who've had dengue before. We don't offer it, so ask a GP or travel doctor.

To avoid bites, use an insect repellent containing DEET, picaridin or oil of lemon eucalyptus, wear loose-fitting, light-coloured clothing, and choose rooms with insect screens or a mosquito net. Always read the label and follow the directions for use.

## Bird flu: avoid live animal markets

Bird flu rarely spreads to people, but the Government of Canada says most human cases worldwide have been reported in China. Avoid live bird and animal markets and poultry farms, don't touch birds, alive or dead, and only eat poultry and eggs that are thoroughly cooked. Your seasonal flu vaccine won't protect you against bird flu.

## Air pollution and lung conditions

The CDC says pollution is still a problem in many parts of China, and people with heart or lung conditions, children and older adults are the most likely to be affected. If you have asthma or another heart or lung condition, talk to your doctor before you go, pack plenty of your regular medicines, and check local air quality before heading out. Ordinary face masks don't filter the fine particles in polluted air, so ask your doctor whether a well-fitted N95 mask suits you.

## Going to Tibet or other high places?

Altitude sickness can start above about 2,500 metres, and Lhasa, in Tibet, sits at 3,658 metres. Watch for a headache, cough, racing heart or trouble breathing. Go up slowly, and once you're above 3,000 metres, climb no more than 500 metres a day. See your doctor before you go, especially if you have heart or lung problems. A doctor can give you medicine to help you adjust, and the CDC says supply in China is unreliable, so take it with you.

## Food and water in China

The CDC says tap water isn't safe to drink in mainland China, even in big cities.

- Drink sealed bottled water or boiled water, and brush your teeth with it too.
- Skip ice. Hot tea and coffee made with boiled water are usually safe.
- Choose food that's freshly cooked and served piping hot, and avoid raw or undercooked meat and seafood.
- Peel fruit yourself, go easy on salads, and wash your hands or use hand sanitiser before you eat.

Pack a small travel health kit with oral rehydration solution, pain relief, antiseptic and insect repellent. See our [travel health checklist](/travel-health-checklist/) for a full countdown to your trip.

## How far ahead should I start?

Ideally 6 to 8 weeks before you fly, as some vaccines need more than one dose or take a couple of weeks to work. Flying out at short notice, perhaps for a family emergency? Come in anyway. A single dose of hepatitis A vaccine still gives protection, and some courses can be sped up. Don't count on finishing a course in China, as the CDC warns some vaccines may be hard to get there.

## When to see a pharmacist or GP

Talk to your pharmacist about which vaccines suit your trip, what to pack, and your regular medicines. Some medicines may not be available in China, and some may not be allowed in, so take enough for the whole trip and check with China's embassy if you're unsure.

See a GP or travel doctor before you go if you're pregnant, have a weakened immune system or a long-term health condition, are travelling with children under 5, or need rabies or dengue vaccine. [Help Medical](/help-medical/), an independent general practice located inside our store, can help.

After you get home, see a doctor if you have a fever, a rash, diarrhoea that doesn't settle, or any animal bite or scratch, and tell them you've been to China. In an emergency, call 000.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need vaccinations to travel to China?

None are required to enter China from Australia. But most travellers should be up to date with measles, tetanus and flu, and talk to a pharmacist about hepatitis A and typhoid vaccines.

### Do I need extra vaccines if I'm visiting family in China?

You may. Family visits are often longer, with more local food and time in people's homes. Typhoid vaccine is especially recommended, and JE and rabies vaccines are worth discussing for long stays or rural trips.

### Is there malaria in China?

No. The World Health Organization certified China malaria-free in 2021, so you don't need malaria tablets for China itself.
