Hand, foot and mouth disease is a common viral infection in young children that causes a fever, a sore mouth and small blisters in the mouth and on the hands and feet. It usually clears up by itself within 7 to 10 days. There's no specific treatment, so care is about keeping your child comfortable and making sure they drink enough. In Queensland, children should stay home from childcare or school until all their blisters have dried.

## What causes hand, foot and mouth disease?

It's caused by a group of viruses, most often a coxsackievirus. It isn't related to foot-and-mouth disease in animals. It's most common in young children, especially in childcare, but adults can catch it too, sometimes with few or no symptoms, and still pass it on.

## What are the symptoms?

- a fever, tiredness and not wanting to eat
- a sore throat and mouth
- small blisters inside the mouth, which can make eating and drinking painful
- small blisters on the hands and feet, and sometimes on the bottom, nappy area, knees or elbows

## How does it spread?

SA Health says it spreads through contact with fluid from the blisters, through coughs and sneezes, and through contact with poo, for example when changing nappies. It can also spread on hands, toys and surfaces.

## How can I look after my child at home?

- **Keep them drinking.** Offer small, frequent sips of water or milk. Cold drinks, icy poles and smooth, soft foods are often easier on a sore mouth. Salty, spicy or acidic food and drinks, like orange juice, can sting.
- **Ease the pain and fever.** Paracetamol or ibuprofen can help, at the right dose for your child's age and weight. Our guide to [paracetamol and ibuprofen for kids](/childrens-paracetamol-ibuprofen/) explains the doses.
- **Leave the blisters alone.** Don't pop them; they'll dry up on their own.
- **Let them rest.**

Check with a pharmacist before using a mouth gel on a child, because some aren't suitable for children. Always read the label and follow the directions for use.

## When can my child go back to childcare or school?

Queensland Health's exclusion guidance is to keep children home until all their blisters have dried. Brothers, sisters and other children who've been in contact don't need to stay home if they're well.

## When should I see a doctor?

The Sydney Children's Hospitals Network advises seeing a doctor promptly if your child:

- has signs of dehydration, such as fewer wet nappies, a dry mouth, or passing very little urine;
- won't drink or has trouble swallowing;
- has a fever of 38°C or more for more than 3 days;
- is very drowsy or hard to wake, has unusual movements, or is breathing fast.

Our guide to [gastro and dehydration in kids](/gastro-in-kids-hydration/) explains the signs of dehydration in more detail. If you're pregnant and have been in contact with someone who has hand, foot and mouth disease, let your doctor or midwife know.

## How can I stop it spreading?

- Wash hands well with soap and water, especially after nappy changes and going to the toilet, and before preparing food. Keep doing it even once your child seems better.
- Don't share cups, cutlery, towels or face washers.
- Clean toys and surfaces your child has touched.
- Teach children to cough and sneeze into their elbow.
- Keep them home until their blisters have dried.

Hand, foot and mouth disease often turns up in the same weeks as [school sores](/school-sores-impetigo/). Our [back to school health checklist](/back-to-school-health-checklist/) covers what else to watch for.

Our pharmacists at Pacific Fair can check the right dose of pain relief for your child, help you choose drinks or an oral rehydration solution if they're not drinking well, and tell you when they need to see a GP.

## When to see a pharmacist or GP

Talk to a pharmacist about pain relief, fluids and keeping your child comfortable. See a GP for any of the warning signs above, or if you're not sure what the rash is. The GPs at [Help Medical](/help-medical/), an independent general practice located inside our store, can help.
