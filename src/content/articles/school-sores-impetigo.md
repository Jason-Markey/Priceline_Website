School sores (impetigo) are a common, contagious skin infection that causes blisters and crusty, honey-coloured sores, usually around the nose and mouth or on the arms and legs. They usually need antibiotic treatment prescribed by a doctor. In Queensland, children with school sores should stay home until they've had antibiotic treatment for at least 24 hours, and any sores on exposed skin should be covered with a waterproof dressing.

## What are school sores?

School sores are a bacterial infection of the top layer of the skin. They're most common in children, and they spread easily in warm, humid weather like a Gold Coast summer. The bacteria often get in through skin that's already broken, such as a scratched insect bite, a graze or a patch of eczema.

## What do school sores look like?

- They often start as small red spots or blisters.
- The blisters break open and weep clear fluid or pus for a few days.
- A flat, crusty, yellow or honey-coloured scab then forms.
- They're most common around the nose and mouth, and on the arms and legs.
- They can be itchy, and scratching spreads them to other parts of the body.

## How do school sores spread?

School sores spread through skin-to-skin contact, and by sharing towels, clothes or bedding. NSW Health says they usually appear 4 to 10 days after contact, and stay infectious for as long as the sores are weeping.

## How are school sores treated?

See a GP if you think your child has school sores. They usually need antibiotic treatment, either a cream or a medicine taken by mouth, depending on how many sores there are and whether your child has a fever. Finish the whole course, even once the sores look better.

At home, healthdirect suggests:

- gently washing the sores with salty water 2 to 3 times a day, then patting them dry;
- covering the sores with waterproof dressings;
- keeping your child's nails short, and discouraging scratching;
- washing hands well after touching the sores;
- giving your child their own towel, and washing their clothes, towels and bedding regularly.

Always read the label and follow the directions for use.

## When can my child go back to school or childcare?

Queensland Health's exclusion guidance is to keep children with school sores home until they've had antibiotic treatment for at least 24 hours. When they go back, cover any sores on exposed skin with a waterproof dressing until they've dried.

Keep a child with school sores away from newborn babies, because the infection can become serious very quickly in a newborn.

## When should I see a doctor urgently?

See a doctor straight away if:

- the sores are spreading quickly, or there are a lot of them;
- your child has a fever or seems unwell;
- the skin around the sores becomes red, swollen, hot or painful, which can be a sign of a deeper skin infection;
- a newborn baby has any sores or blisters.

## How can I prevent school sores?

- Clean and cover cuts, grazes and scratched insect bites. Our guides to [treating a cut](/how-to-treat-a-cut/) and [bites and stings](/bites-and-stings-first-aid/) explain how.
- Keep eczema under control, so the skin doesn't crack. See our guide to [eczema in summer](/eczema-summer/).
- Wash hands often, especially after playing outside.
- Don't share towels, clothes or bedding.
- Bath or shower your child every day.

School sores are one of several things that do the rounds at the start of the school year. Our [back to school health checklist](/back-to-school-health-checklist/) covers the others, including [head lice](/head-lice-treatment/).
