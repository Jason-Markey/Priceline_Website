Most medicines should be stored below 25°C, out of direct sun, in their original packaging. On the Gold Coast that's harder than it sounds from November to March. A parked car, a beach bag or a sunny kitchen windowsill can all get well above that, and heat can change how well a medicine works.

Our pharmacists at Pacific Fair answer questions about this every summer, so here are the ones we hear most, with practical answers for life in Broadbeach and beyond.

## What temperature should medicines be stored at?

Check the label first, because it's the rule for that product. As a general guide, the Pharmaceutical Society of Australia's journal says most medicines should be stored below 25°C. Manufacturers test their products at higher temperatures too, so a short spell of warmth isn't automatically a disaster, but regular or prolonged heat is best avoided.

Medicines labelled "refrigerate" belong in the fridge, not the freezer and not the door, where the temperature swings every time it opens.

## Is it OK to leave medicines in the car?

No. Pharmacy experts warn that even a short time in a car parked in direct sunlight can be a problem. On a summer day, the inside of a parked car can get much hotter than the air outside. That includes the glovebox, the console and the boot.

If you're heading to the beach, the shops or a theme park, take your medicines with you in a bag, ideally an insulated one. And Queensland Health's most important car rule applies to people, not just pills: never leave children, older people or pets in a parked car, even for a few minutes.

## Which medicines are most sensitive to heat?

Liquids and solutions are generally more heat sensitive than tablets. The ones we worry about most in summer are:

- **Injectable medicines**, including diabetes medicines kept in the fridge.
- **Asthma puffers and allergy autoinjectors.** Heat can affect the medicine and the device itself, and these are the medicines you need to work in an emergency.
- **Liquid medicines**, creams and suppositories, which can separate or melt.
- **Anything labelled "refrigerate"**, such as some eye drops, antibiotic mixtures and probiotics.

If you're not sure whether yours is heat sensitive, ask us. It takes a minute to check.

## How can I tell if a medicine has been damaged by heat?

Look for changes from how it normally looks. The warning signs include:

- a change in colour, smell or consistency;
- tablets or capsules that are soft, sticky, cracked or chipped;
- powders that have clumped together;
- creams that have separated, or suppositories that have melted and reset.

Some heat damage can't be seen at all. If a medicine has been somewhere very hot, especially an emergency medicine, bring it in and ask a pharmacist before you rely on it. If it needs replacing, we can dispose of the old one safely. See [how to dispose of old medicines](/how-to-dispose-of-old-medicines/).

## How do I carry medicines to the beach or on a day out?

Keep it simple:

- Take only what you need for the day, in the original packaging with the pharmacy label.
- Use an insulated bag, and keep it in the shade, not on the sand.
- For medicines that must stay cold, use a cooler bag with an ice pack, but wrap the medicine so it doesn't touch the ice pack directly and freeze.
- Keep emergency medicines, like a reliever puffer or an allergy autoinjector, with the person who needs them, not in the car or back at the hotel.

## What about medicines in dose packs or blister packs?

Dose administration aids, like the packs we prepare through our [medication packing service](/medication-packing/), need the same care. Keep packs in a cool, dry cupboard away from the stove, kettle and windows, and don't leave a week's supply in a hot car. If a pack has been exposed to heat or moisture and the medicines look different, bring it in so we can check it.

## Can some medicines make the heat harder to handle?

Yes. Queensland Health says some medicines can increase the risk of heat-related illness, including some allergy medicines, fluid tablets, some blood pressure medicines, and antidepressant and antipsychotic medicines. Some medicines affect how much you sweat or how thirsty you feel, and dehydration can change how others work in the body.

Don't stop a regular medicine because of the weather. Instead, ask your pharmacist or doctor whether yours needs any extra care in hot weather, drink water regularly, and know the [signs of heat exhaustion](/heat-exhaustion-symptoms/). A [medication review](/medication-reviews/) is a good time to go through everything you take.

## I'm travelling over summer. How should I pack my medicines?

Pack your medicines in your carry-on bag rather than checked luggage, because cargo holds and luggage left on the tarmac can get hot. Bring enough for the trip plus a few spare days, keep them in their original packaging with the pharmacy label, and carry a list of what you take. If you use diabetes supplies, we're an access point for the [National Diabetes Services Scheme](/national-diabetes-services-scheme/). Our [travel health checklist](/travel-health-checklist/) covers the rest, and our [summer first aid kit checklist](/first-aid-kit-checklist/) is handy for road trips.
