Stock a few basics before your child gets sick: a digital thermometer, children's paracetamol and ibuprofen in the right strength with their measuring syringe, oral rehydration solution, saline nose drops, a nappy rash cream and some first aid supplies. Add a card with key phone numbers and your child's current weight, and keep everything up high and out of sight.

Fevers and tummy bugs have a habit of starting in the middle of the night. Here's a checklist to tick off now, so you're not hunting for basics at 2am.

## What should be in a children's medicine cabinet?

### For fever and pain

- **A digital thermometer.** The Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne (RCH) suggests measuring your child's temperature under the arm with a digital thermometer. Ear thermometers can be used too, but the RCH doesn't recommend them for babies under six months. It says forehead infrared and plastic-tape thermometers can be unreliable for children, and to always follow the thermometer's instructions. The thermometers we sell come with instructions, in a booklet or online, so have a read before you need it. See the [thermometer range on priceline.com.au](https://www.priceline.com.au/c/thermometers).
- **Children's paracetamol and ibuprofen.** healthdirect says children's medicines often come in different strengths for different age groups, so check the strength is right for your child's age and weight. It notes paracetamol is for babies over 1 month and ibuprofen for babies over 3 months. Our guide to [paracetamol and ibuprofen for kids](/childrens-paracetamol-ibuprofen/) covers doses, timing and who shouldn't have ibuprofen.
- **The measuring syringe or cup.** healthdirect says to always use the one that comes with a liquid medicine. If you've lost it, ask your pharmacist for another.

The RCH defines a fever as 38°C or more, and says it's more important to watch your child for other symptoms than to keep rechecking their temperature.

### For tummy bugs

- **Oral rehydration solution.** The RCH describes oral rehydration fluids as replacing lost fluids and body salts, and the best option if your child is dehydrated. They also come as icy poles, which some children find easier to take. See our guide to [keeping kids hydrated with gastro](/gastro-in-kids-hydration/).

### For colds and blocked noses

- **Saline nose drops or spray.** Saline can help with a blocked nose. It's salt water, which is different from a medicated spray: healthdirect says cough and cold medicines, including nasal sprays, aren't suitable for children under 6.

### For skin

- **A barrier cream for nappy rash.** The RCH advises a thick layer of barrier cream, so you can't see the skin through it, at every nappy change.
- **Sunscreen, for babies 6 months and older.** Cancer Council says widespread sunscreen use on babies under six months isn't generally recommended. For older babies, sunscreen is the last line of defence after shade, clothing and a hat. See [sun protection for babies](/baby-sun-protection/).
- **Insect repellent, for babies 3 months and older.** The Queensland Government says repellent isn't recommended for babies under 3 months (use mosquito nets over prams and cots instead), and children under 5 shouldn't put it on themselves. It says to put repellent on your own hands first, then on your child's exposed skin. More in our guide to [mosquito bites and Ross River virus](/mosquito-bites-ross-river-virus/).

### For cuts, scrapes and bumps

- **Non-stick dressings and plasters.** The RCH says a non-stick dressing is a good option for grazes, and a plaster works for most cuts, as long as the sticky part stays off the cut. See [how to treat a cut](/how-to-treat-a-cut/).
- **Saline tubes, small scissors and tweezers** for cleaning and dressing small wounds.
- **An instant cold pack** for bumps and sprains. Wrap it in a light cloth rather than putting it straight on the skin.

For a family kit for the beach, pool and car, see our [summer first aid kit checklist](/first-aid-kit-checklist/). You can also browse the [children's medicine](https://www.priceline.com.au/c/childrens-medicine) and [baby health](https://www.priceline.com.au/c/baby-health) ranges on priceline.com.au. Always read the label and follow the directions for use.

## What should go on your emergency card?

Write these on a card and stick it inside the cabinet door or on the fridge:

- **000** for emergencies
- **13 HEALTH (13 43 25 84)** to talk to a registered nurse any time, day or night, in Queensland
- **Poisons Information Centre: 13 11 26.** The Queensland Poisons Information Centre says it's available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, from anywhere in Australia
- your GP's phone number
- **your child's current weight**, and the date you checked it

The weight matters. healthdirect says doctors use body weight to work out the right amount of medicine, and the RCH says to check the dose for your child's weight on the pack, as the ages listed are only a guide.

healthdirect also suggests writing down each dose you give: the medicine's name, the time, the amount and the active ingredient.

## How do you store children's medicines safely?

Kidsafe says most accidental poisonings happen in children under five, with toddlers at the greatest risk. The Queensland Poisons Information Centre recommends:

- **Up high and out of sight.** Store medicines at least 1.5 metres high, out of reach and out of sight of children.
- **Locked away.** Use a locked or child-resistant cupboard.
- **Child-resistant packaging.** Buy medicines in child-resistant packaging, but remember Kidsafe's reminder that child-resistant doesn't mean child-proof.
- **Original containers.** Keep medicines in the containers they came in.
- **Proper names.** Call medicines by their proper names. They are not lollies.
- **Visitors' bags.** Bags can hold medicines, so keep visitors' bags out of reach.

Check storage instructions too: healthdirect notes some medicines must go in the fridge.

## What shouldn't be in the cabinet?

- **Expired medicines.** Medicines shouldn't be used after their expiry date, so check the dates every time you restock.
- **Adult medicines and other people's medicines.** Only give your child medicines made for children, at the dose for their weight, and never medicine prescribed for someone else.
- **Aspirin.** healthdirect says it shouldn't be given to children under 12 unless a doctor specifically prescribes it.

Take anything old or unwanted to a pharmacy. We take back unwanted medicines for free at Pacific Fair; see [how to get rid of old medicines safely](/how-to-dispose-of-old-medicines/).

## When to see a pharmacist or GP

If your baby is under 3 months old and has a fever above 38°C, see a doctor straight away. healthdirect says to take them to the nearest hospital emergency department immediately, and the RCH says a baby under 1 month with a fever should go to hospital.

Talk to a pharmacist if you're unsure about a product, strength or dose. See a GP (the GPs at [Help Medical](/help-medical/), an independent general practice located inside our store, are one option) if:

- your child seems to be getting sicker, or isn't getting better after two days
- nappy rash doesn't improve within a week, or is severe
- a cut shows signs of infection, such as redness, swelling, ooze or pus
- you're worried. You know your child best.

If your child has swallowed a medicine, or too much of one, call the Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26 straight away. Don't wait for symptoms, and don't try to make them vomit.

**Call 000 in an emergency.** Call triple zero if your child has collapsed or stopped breathing, is struggling to breathe, looks blue or very pale, or has a seizure that lasts more than five minutes.

**Need advice after hours?** In Queensland, call 13 HEALTH (13 43 25 84) any time to talk to a registered nurse. Outside Queensland, call healthdirect on 1800 022 222.
