Warts are small, rough skin growths caused by a virus, and plantar warts (verrucas) are warts on the soles of the feet. Many go away on their own: the Better Health Channel says about 65% clear within two years without treatment. If a wart bothers you, a wart paint or gel containing salicylic acid from the pharmacy is the usual first step, used every day for several weeks. See a GP if you have diabetes, if the wart is on your face or genitals, or if it bleeds, changes or keeps coming back.

## What causes warts?

Warts are caused by types of human papillomavirus (HPV) that infect the skin. They spread through direct contact, or from surfaces where the virus survives, such as pool decks, change rooms and shared showers. Soft, wet or broken skin is easier for the virus to get into.

## Common warts and plantar warts

- **Common warts** are rough, raised bumps, usually on the hands, fingers or knees.
- **Plantar warts (verrucas)** grow on the soles of the feet. Walking presses them flat and hard, often with tiny black dots in the middle, and they can hurt when you walk.

A hard, painful spot on the foot can also be a corn or callus. A pharmacist or podiatrist can tell you which it is.

## Do warts go away on their own?

Often, yes, but it can take months or years. The Better Health Channel suggests not waiting if you have several warts, or if you've had a wart for more than two years.

## Which wart treatments work?

The Australasian College of Dermatologists says salicylic acid and freezing are the most common and effective treatments, and that they often need repeating.

**Salicylic acid paints, gels and plasters** soften and gradually remove the wart.

- Soak the wart in warm water for a few minutes, then gently file the surface with an emery board or foot file kept just for the wart.
- Apply the treatment to the wart only, protecting the skin around it.
- Repeat every day. It usually takes many weeks, often around 12, to work.
- Stop and ask a pharmacist if the skin around the wart becomes red or sore.

**Freezing.** Freezing kits are available from pharmacies, and your GP can freeze a wart with liquid nitrogen if home treatments don't work. Freezing can sting, and it often takes more than one treatment.

Always read the label and follow the directions for use, including the age limits.

## Who shouldn't treat warts at home?

See a GP or podiatrist before treating a wart yourself if:

- you have diabetes, poor circulation or reduced feeling in your feet;
- the wart is on your face or genitals (genital warts need different treatment);
- you're pregnant, or have a weakened immune system;
- you're not sure it's a wart.

## How can I stop warts spreading?

- Wear thongs at public pools, change rooms and shared showers.
- Don't pick or scratch warts, and cover them with a plaster at the pool.
- Don't share towels, socks or shoes.
- Use a separate file or pumice stone on a wart, and don't use it anywhere else.
- Keep your feet clean and dry. Our guides to [athlete's foot](/athletes-foot-treatment/) and [blisters and summer foot care](/blisters-summer-foot-care/) have more foot care tips.

## When to see a pharmacist or GP

Talk to a pharmacist about common warts and verrucas. See a GP if a wart bleeds, changes colour or shape, spreads quickly or is painful, or if it hasn't improved after about 12 weeks of treatment.
