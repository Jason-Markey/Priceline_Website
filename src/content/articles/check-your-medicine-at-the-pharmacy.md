Before you leave the counter, take a moment to check your medicine. Look for your name, the medicine's name and strength, the directions, the quantity and the expiry date. If anything looks different from last time, ask before you go.

There's often a simple explanation, such as a different brand, and it's much easier to sort out at the counter than at home. When your script is ready, we call your name, and that's the perfect time to check. Here's what to look for, how to read the label and the box, and how to keep your scripts safe.

## What should I check before I leave?

A quick checklist:

- **Your name.** Make sure it's your name on the label, or the name of the person you're collecting for.
- **The medicine and its strength.** Check they match what your doctor told you to expect.
- **The directions.** How much to take, how often and when. If they're unclear, ask.
- **The quantity.** We supply the full quantity on your script, not part of it. If we're short of stock, we tell you when you hand your script in. You can then have us order it in, or take your script elsewhere.
- **The expiry date.** The TGA explains it's shown as month and year, so 02/27 means February 2027. Medicines shouldn't be used after their expiry date.
- **Whether it looks different.** A new box, or a tablet of a different colour or shape, may be a different brand of the same medicine. Our guide to [generic vs brand-name medicines](/generic-vs-brand-medicines/) explains why they can look different. Check the active ingredient and strength still match, and ask if you're unsure.

## How do I read the pharmacy label?

The pharmacy label is the sticker added when your medicine is dispensed. The Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care notes it may be the only place you receive personalised instructions for your medicine.

The Commission's national standard for labelling dispensed medicines says a label should include:

- your name
- the active ingredient and the brand name
- the strength and form, such as tablets or liquid
- clear dosing instructions
- the date it was dispensed
- the pharmacy's name and address

Some labels also show what the medicine is for, the most you should take for a medicine used "when needed", or a date to throw it away. If anything on the label doesn't make sense, ask us to go through it with you.

## What should I look for on the box?

The box from the manufacturer has its own label. The TGA says to read a medicine label carefully before you use any medicine, and follow all instructions. Look for:

- **Active ingredients.** The label shows the name and amount of each active ingredient.
- **Warnings.** These tell you when you shouldn't use the medicine.
- **Storage.** How to store the medicine.
- **Expiry date and batch number.** The batch number lets the medicine's production history be traced.
- **The AUST R or AUST L number.** This is the medicine's number on the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods.

Read the active ingredient, not just the brand name. Two products with different names can contain the same active ingredient, so it's possible to take too much by accident.

Keep your medicines in their original containers, with the labels on. Better Health Channel notes the labels include expiry dates and storage instructions.

## What's in the Consumer Medicine Information leaflet?

healthdirect explains that the Consumer Medicine Information (CMI) leaflet gives you facts to know before, during and after taking your medicine. It covers things like how to take it, what to do if you miss a dose, possible interactions and how to store it.

All prescription medicines and some non-prescription medicines have a CMI. You'll find it in the medicine packaging, or you can ask your pharmacist for it. Better Health Channel suggests reading the CMI and all the labels carefully before you use a medicine.

## How do I keep my scripts safe?

Lost scripts cause a lot of last-minute stress. A few habits help:

- **eScript tokens.** The Australian Digital Health Agency suggests checking you've received your token before you leave the doctor. If you have repeats, the pharmacy sends you a new token by SMS or email to use next time. Keep it until you need it.
- **Lost a token?** You can ask for it to be resent. For a new script, contact the doctor who wrote it. For a repeat, contact the pharmacy that supplied it last time.
- **An Active Script List.** This keeps your eScripts in one place, so you don't need to keep track of tokens. We can register you in store.
- **Paper repeats.** We can keep your paper repeats on file, so they can't get lost at home.

Our guide to [eScripts, Active Script Lists and scripts on file](/how-to-use-escripts/) explains each option.

## What if something looks wrong?

If something doesn't look right, check with us before you take it, and call us straight away if it's a dose you can't miss. To sort it out:

1. Come back to the prescription counter, or [contact us](/contact-us/).
2. Bring the medicine, with its box and label.
3. Tell us what looks different.

Often it's a simple change, such as a new brand. If it isn't, the pharmacist can work out what needs to happen next.

### What if I've already taken it?

If you've taken the wrong medicine, someone else's medicine or the wrong dose, call the Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26. The Queensland Poisons Information Centre gives advice 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, from anywhere in Australia.

Have the medicine container with you when you call. If you think someone has taken too much, don't wait for symptoms. If the person has collapsed or isn't breathing, call 000.

## Why keep a current medicines list?

healthdirect says medicine lists help make sure everyone involved in your health care knows what you're taking. Its advice is to list:

- every medicine, prescription and non-prescription
- what each one is for
- the dose
- when and how to take it

Keep the list on paper or in an app, and take it to appointments. Better Health Channel suggests carrying a list of your medicines and their dosage instructions in case of an emergency. Update it whenever a medicine changes.

If you take several medicines, a [medication review](/medication-reviews/) with one of our pharmacists is a good time to check your list. [Webster packs](/medication-packing/) can also help, by sorting your doses by day and time.

## When to see a pharmacist or GP

Talk to your pharmacist or GP if:

- you notice new side effects after starting a medicine, or after a change of brand
- you're not sure whether a medicine is the right one
- you think you may have taken two products with the same active ingredient
- you've missed doses or doubled up, and aren't sure what to do next

If someone has taken the wrong medicine or too much, call the Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26.

**Call 000 in an emergency.** If someone has collapsed, isn't breathing or is having a severe allergic reaction, call triple zero (000) straight away.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does my medicine look different this time?

It may be a different brand of the same medicine. Check that the active ingredient and strength match what you usually take, and ask us if you're unsure. Never take two brands of the same medicine together unless you've been told to.

### Can I use a medicine after its expiry date?

No. The TGA says medicines should not be used after their expiry date. Bring out-of-date medicines back to us for safe disposal.
