The SPF in your foundation helps, but it shouldn't be your only sun protection. University of Queensland (UQ) researchers say SPF 30 mixed into foundation is not going to be as effective as a primary SPF 30 sunscreen, and Cancer Council's advice is to apply a dedicated sunscreen first, on clean, dry skin, with your makeup on top.

That matters on the Gold Coast, where UV reaches 3 or above every day of the year, even on the walk from the car park into Pacific Fair. Below, our beauty team and pharmacists explain why makeup SPF falls short, what the TGA means by primary and secondary sunscreens, and how to layer and top up without wrecking your base.

## Why isn't the SPF in my foundation enough?

It comes down to how much you apply. Sunscreens are tested with a thick, even layer. The UQ researchers, writing in The Conversation, put the equivalent amount for your face, ears and neck at about one teaspoon (5 mL), the same guide Cancer Council uses. Picture a full teaspoon of foundation spread over your face, ears and neck: that's the kind of amount it would take. The researchers also point out that powder is generally applied lightly.

Cancer Council NSW adds that cosmetics containing sunscreen vary in how much protection they give, and it can often be very little.

### Do SPFs add up if you layer products?

No. Cancer Council gives the example of an SPF20 moisturiser worn with an SPF10 foundation. Together they don't make SPF30. You're only protected to the level of the highest SPF product, which in this case is SPF20.

### When is makeup with SPF enough on its own?

Cancer Council says a cosmetic with SPF30 is adequate to protect against sunburn if you're indoors with only incidental sun exposure, but, like sunscreen, it's only effective for two hours. If you're going to be outdoors, Cancer Council recommends sunscreen rather than a moisturiser containing SPF. healthdirect puts it plainly: makeup should not be used instead of sunscreen.

## What's the difference between a primary and a secondary sunscreen?

The Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) sorts products by what they're mainly for.

- **Primary sunscreens** are products mainly for protecting your skin from UV. They're therapeutic goods, generally carry an AUST L number on the label, and must meet the testing and labelling requirements of the Australian sunscreen standard.
- **Secondary sunscreens** have another main job, with SPF as an extra. Tinted bases and foundations (liquids, pastes or powders) with SPF 4 or more, lip products with SPF 4 or more, and moisturisers with an SPF between 4 and 15 that meet certain conditions are excluded from therapeutic goods rules and sold as cosmetics. They still have to meet the testing and labelling requirements of the Australian standard. Moisturisers with an SPF over 15 stay in the therapeutic group.

The practical takeaway: check for an AUST L number on whatever you're relying on as your base layer of protection, and treat the SPF in your foundation, powder or lipstick as a bonus.

## How do you apply sunscreen under makeup?

1. **Start with clean, dry skin.** Cancer Council recommends applying sunscreen first, before moisturiser or makeup. Cancer Council NSW explains this allows the sunscreen to disperse effectively.
2. **Use enough.** Aim for about a teaspoon across your face, neck and ears, and don't skip your hairline and the backs of your ears.
3. **Let it set.** The UQ researchers suggest giving sunscreen a few minutes to dry and sink into the skin before you put on other products. Cancer Council NSW recommends applying it 20 minutes before you go outside, which gives it time to bind to your skin.
4. **Add moisturiser if you need it.** If your sunscreen feels hydrating enough, you may not need a separate moisturiser on humid days. If you use a mineral (physical) sunscreen, the UQ researchers suggest moisturiser can go on first, then sunscreen, then makeup.
5. **Press, don't drag.** Our beauty team's tip: stipple or press foundation on with a damp sponge or brush rather than rubbing it across your face, so both layers stay even.

## How do you reapply sunscreen over makeup?

Makeup doesn't change the rule. Reapply every two hours and after swimming, sweating or towelling. It's the step that's easiest to skip, so here are your options and their limits.

| Option | How to do it | The catch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Start fresh | Remove your makeup, reapply sunscreen generously, then redo your base | The best protection, but rarely practical mid-day |
| Pat sunscreen over makeup | Blot any shine, then press a thin layer of sunscreen on with a clean sponge or fingertips | Takes patience; the UQ researchers say mineral sunscreens work best this way |
| SPF powder | Press it over your face | Powder goes on lightly and tinted powders are cosmetics, so treat it as a top-up for incidental sun, not protection for a day outdoors |
| Spray or mist | Spray over your face | healthdirect doesn't recommend spray sunscreens because it's hard to apply enough, and Cancer Council strongly advises against aerosols |
| Sunscreen stick | Glide over your nose, cheekbones and ears | Small areas only, and it can shift makeup, so go slowly |

For a beach day or an afternoon at the theme parks, start fresh, or plan a lighter makeup look around your sunscreen.

## Which sunscreen textures work under makeup in humid weather?

In a sticky Gold Coast summer, the sunscreen you're happy to wear every day is the one that protects you.

- **Expecting to sweat?** Cancer Council suggests trying a dry-touch formula.
- **Experiment with finishes.** The UQ researchers suggest trying features like a matte finish, a milk texture or a fragrance-free formula until you find one you like.
- **Sensitive skin?** Cancer Council suggests fragrance-free and paraben-free products.
- **Check the label.** Cancer Council recommends SPF50 or SPF50+, broad spectrum and water-resistant. A sunscreen is only water-resistant for the time stated on the label, so keep reapplying. Wondering whether the jump from SPF30 matters? See [SPF30 vs SPF50](/spf30-vs-spf50/).
- **Tinted products.** If you like a tint, check whether it's a primary sunscreen with an AUST L number or a tinted foundation with SPF, which is a cosmetic.
- **Keep it cool.** Cancer Council says to store sunscreen below 30°C in a cool, dark place, and ideally not in a car on a hot day.

Sunscreen is one part of sun protection. Whenever the UV index is 3 or above, pair it with clothing, a broad-brimmed hat, shade and sunglasses: slip, slop, slap, seek and slide. ## When to see a pharmacist or GP

Talk to a pharmacist if a sunscreen or makeup product stings, burns or brings you out in a rash. Stop using it and bring it in so we can help you find an alternative. See a GP (our in-store [Help Medical GPs](/help-medical/) are one option) if:

- a spot or mole is new, changing or looks different from your others
- you notice new patches of pigmentation on your face
- a rash from a product spreads, blisters or doesn't settle after you stop using it
- you have sunburn with large areas of blistering or severe pain
