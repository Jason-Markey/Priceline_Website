A dry cough is tickly and brings nothing up. A chesty cough brings up mucus, also called phlegm. The difference matters when you choose a medicine: cough suppressants are meant for a dry cough, while expectorants and mucolytics are meant for a chesty one. Most coughs from a cold settle on their own, and healthdirect says this kind of cough normally goes away within 2 to 4 weeks in healthy people.

Here's how to tell the two apart, what helps, why some cough medicines you may remember are no longer on the shelf, and when to see a doctor.

## How can I tell a dry cough from a chesty cough?

A quick guide, based on healthdirect's descriptions (the medicine classes are explained below):

| | Dry cough | Chesty cough |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Also called | Non-productive | Productive or wet |
| What you notice | A tickle or irritation in your throat | Mucus you cough up, sometimes with a tight or congested chest |
| Mucus | None | Usually white or clear |
| Medicine class meant for it | Cough suppressants | Expectorants and mucolytics |

## What causes a cough?

healthdirect says cold and flu viruses are the most common cause of a short-term cough. Others include:

- **Post-nasal drip:** mucus dripping down your throat from the back of your nose or sinuses.
- **Asthma:** look out for wheezing, shortness of breath and chest tightness.
- **Reflux (GORD):** stomach acid comes up and causes irritation. See our guide to [heartburn relief](/heartburn-relief/).
- **Smoking and vaping,** including breathing other people's smoke. Our [quit smoking](/quit-smoking/) page can help.
- **Some medicines,** including some blood pressure medicines. If you suspect a medicine, see your doctor, as a different one may be available.

### How long does a cough last after a cold?

The Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne (RCH) notes it's common for a child's dry cough to continue for several weeks after a cold, which is called a post-viral cough. But healthdirect says coughing that lasts for more than 3 weeks may be a sign of a health problem, so see your GP if yours hasn't settled by then.

## What helps a cough at home?

- **Drink plenty of water and rest.**
- **Try honey,** off a spoon or stirred into a warm drink. healthdirect says it may help soothe your throat and reduce irritation. Never give honey to babies under 12 months: the RCH warns of the risk of botulism.
- **Breathe in steam from hot water,** taking care not to scald yourself. This one isn't for children: healthdirect says steam isn't recommended for croup, as there's no evidence it helps and it can cause burns.
- **Skip caffeine and alcohol,** which can dry out your throat.
- **Avoid smoke and vapes,** including other people's.

For a blocked nose, sore throat and the rest of a cold, see our [common cold remedies](/common-cold-remedies/).

## Which cough medicine suits a dry or chesty cough?

Over-the-counter cough medicines come in a few classes. healthdirect advises checking with your pharmacist before you take a cough suppressant or give one to your child.

### For a dry cough: cough suppressants

healthdirect explains that cough suppressants reduce your urge to cough by blocking the cough reflex in your brain. They can cause drowsiness, constipation, and nausea and vomiting. Don't drive or operate heavy machinery after taking one, and avoid alcohol, which can add to the drowsiness.

### For a chesty cough: expectorants and mucolytics

Monash University pharmacy researcher Dr Janet Sluggett explains that expectorants are meant to help loosen mucus so you can cough it up, while mucolytics may thin the mucus in your airways. She advises against using a cough suppressant for a chesty, productive cough. After all, coughing is how your body clears your airways.

### Do cough medicines actually work?

Not as well as most people hope. healthdirect says there isn't much evidence that cough medicines help a cough clear up sooner, although they may ease some symptoms. Many cold and flu products combine several ingredients, and some night-time ones contain ingredients that cause drowsiness, so check with a pharmacist before combining products. Always read the label and follow the directions for use.

## Can children have cough medicine?

healthdirect says cough and cold medicines should not be given to children under 6, and that children should only have them if a doctor or pharmacist advises it.

The RCH adds that there's no evidence cough medicines help children with a cough, and that they can be harmful, especially for children under six. Rest, fluids and honey (for children over 12 months) are a better place to start.

The Queensland Government notes that most whooping cough hospitalisations and deaths are in babies under 6 months. Our guide to the [whooping cough vaccine in pregnancy](/whooping-cough-vaccine-pregnancy/) explains how to protect a newborn.

## Why can't I buy the cough medicine I used to?

If a cough or cold medicine you remember is no longer available, one of two changes is usually the reason.

- **2018: some became prescription only.** From 1 February 2018, a group of over-the-counter pain relievers and cough and cold medicines became prescription only. The TGA pointed to substantial evidence of harm from misuse, and little evidence they worked better than products without that ingredient. It says safer, more effective medicines are available that may relieve coughs and colds.
- **2023: some were taken off the market.** The TGA cancelled the registration of a group of medicines used for dry coughs, and remaining stock was recalled from pharmacies. This followed a suspected link to potentially life-threatening allergic reactions (anaphylaxis) in people having a general anaesthetic with certain muscle relaxants.

So if you can't find a product you remember, it may need a prescription now or may no longer be sold in Australia. Your pharmacist will ask who the medicine is for, what the cough is like and what else you take, so they can suggest something that suits.

## When to see a pharmacist or GP

See a GP if your cough comes with any of the following. The GPs at [Help Medical](/help-medical/), an independent general practice located inside our store, are one option.

- It has lasted more than 3 weeks.
- You cough up blood, or your phlegm is green, yellow or bloody.
- You're short of breath or wheezing, or you have chest pain.
- You have a high fever, or unexplained weight loss or tiredness.
- Coughing is stopping you from sleeping.
- You think a medicine may be causing it.

For a child, see a doctor promptly if they:

- have a barking cough or noisy, high-pitched breathing (stridor), especially when they're resting and not crying, or the skin between their ribs pulls in as they breathe
- have bouts of coughing that end in a "whoop", gagging or vomiting. Babies may not whoop at all
- are under 6 months old and have signs of croup
- have had a cough for more than 4 weeks, or seem unwell or unusually tired

If someone has taken too much cough medicine, or a child has got into it, call the Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26.

**Call 000 in an emergency.** Call triple zero if someone is struggling to breathe or can't speak without gasping, has chest pain that won't go away, is drowsy or not responding, or has pale, blue or grey lips. That includes a child who turns blue or stops breathing during a coughing fit.

**Need advice after hours?** In Queensland, call 13 HEALTH (13 43 25 84) any time to talk to a registered nurse. Outside Queensland, call healthdirect on 1800 022 222.
