At the pharmacy, as anywhere you receive health care in Australia, you can expect respect and privacy. You can also ask questions and share in decisions about your care. The Australian Charter of Healthcare Rights sets out what you can expect.

In practice, that means you never have to discuss your health in front of a queue. You can usually choose a generic or the brand, and you can choose where to fill your scripts. Here's what the Charter says, how your health information is protected, and what to do if something doesn't feel right.

## What is the Australian Charter of Healthcare Rights?

The Charter was developed by the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care. It describes the rights that you, or someone you care for, can expect when receiving health care. The Commission says these rights apply to all people in all places where health care is provided in Australia.

The Charter lists seven rights:

- **Access:** health care services and treatment that meet your needs.
- **Safety:** safe, high-quality care that meets national standards, in an environment that is safe and makes you feel safe.
- **Respect:** to be treated as an individual, with dignity and respect, and to have your culture, identity, beliefs and choices recognised and respected.
- **Partnership:** to ask questions and be involved in open and honest communication. You can make decisions with your healthcare provider, to the extent you choose and are able to, and include the people you want.
- **Information:** clear information about your condition and the possible benefits and risks of tests and treatments, so you can give informed consent. It also covers help to understand health information, access to your health information, and being told if something goes wrong.
- **Privacy:** to have your personal privacy respected, and information about you and your health kept secure and confidential.
- **Give feedback:** to give feedback or make a complaint without it affecting the way you're treated. It also includes having your concerns addressed in a transparent and timely way, and sharing your experience to help improve care.

## Do I have to explain my symptoms at the register?

No. You don't have to name a condition, or say why you need a medicine, in front of other customers. Just ask to talk to a pharmacist privately.

We have a private consultation room at Pacific Fair. Anyone can ask to talk to a pharmacist there, and you don't need an appointment. Some conversations are simply easier away from the counter, whether it's a new script, a sensitive symptom or a medicine you're unsure about.

## How is my health information protected?

The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) explains that health information is "sensitive information" under the Privacy Act. That means some stricter requirements apply when it's handled. The OAIC lists pharmacists among the health service providers covered by the Privacy Act. Its examples of health information include prescriptions and other pharmaceutical purchases.

The OAIC also says:

- a health service provider usually needs your consent to collect your health information
- they should explain why they're collecting it, how they'll store and protect it, and who they may disclose it to
- they must have a privacy policy, and you can ask for it at any time
- your health information can't be used for direct marketing unless you specifically agree

Our [privacy policy](/privacy-policy/) explains how we handle your information.

### Can I see the information the pharmacy holds about me?

You can ask. The OAIC says you have a general right to request access to the health information a health service provider holds about you. The provider should respond within a reasonable period, and the OAIC generally considers 30 days reasonable.

A provider can refuse in some situations, for example if access could put someone's life, health or safety at risk. If they refuse, the OAIC says they must give you a written notice explaining why and how you can complain.

## Can I choose a generic or the brand?

Yes, in most cases. When you hand over a script, you can choose a generic or the brand. The exception is when your doctor has marked it "brand substitution not permitted". Our guide to [generic vs brand-name medicines](/generic-vs-brand-medicines/) explains how they compare.

If you'd like to stay on the brand you know, or try a generic, say so when you hand over your script. And if your medicine has changed and you weren't expecting it, ask. The Charter's Partnership right is about making decisions with your healthcare provider, to the extent you choose.

## Can I choose where to fill my prescriptions?

Yes. The Australian Digital Health Agency explains that with an eScript, you visit the pharmacy of your choice and present your token, or send it ahead. With an Active Script List, a pharmacy can dispense your eScripts once you give it your consent.

If a pharmacy keeps your paper repeats on file and you'd like to use another pharmacy, ask for them back. Our guides explain [eScripts and Active Script Lists](/how-to-use-escripts/) and [moving your prescriptions to a new pharmacy](/transfer-prescriptions-new-pharmacy/), step by step.

## Can I ask questions about my medicine?

Always. The Charter includes the right to ask questions, and to clear information about the possible benefits and risks of your treatment. Even when the counter is busy, take your time, or ask to talk privately. Questions worth asking include:

- How much should I take, how often, and at what times?
- Should I take it with food?
- What should I do if I miss a dose?
- How long should I take it for?
- What side effects should I watch for?
- Does it interact with my other medicines?

You can also ask for the Consumer Medicine Information (CMI) leaflet. healthdirect explains a CMI has information about your medicine and how to take it safely. All prescription medicines and some non-prescription medicines have one. You'll find it in the medicine packaging, or you can ask your pharmacist for it.

## What if I'm not happy with how I was treated?

The Charter gives you the right to give feedback or make a complaint without it affecting the way you're treated. You also have the right to have your concerns addressed in a transparent and timely way.

Start by talking to the pharmacist in charge. Many concerns can be sorted out on the spot, and we'd always rather know. You can talk to us in store or [contact us](/contact-us/).

If it isn't resolved, you can contact Queensland's Office of the Health Ombudsman (OHO). The OHO describes itself as Queensland's health service complaints agency and an independent statutory authority. It's the single entry point for complaints about health practitioners, health services and health care facilities in Queensland.

Before you complain to the OHO, it suggests trying to talk with your health service provider first, where that's appropriate. You can make a complaint online through the OHO website or by calling 133 OHO (133 646).

For a concern about how your personal information was handled, the OAIC says you can lodge a complaint with it.

## How our pharmacists can help

Just ask at the counter. Our pharmacists can:

- talk with you privately in our consultation room, with no appointment needed
- explain your generic and brand options when you hand over a script
- go through how to take a new medicine, including its CMI
- register you for an Active Script List in store

You can also send your eScript ahead by email, the Priceline Pharmacy app or MedAdvisor. Our [prescriptions page](/prescriptions/) explains how.

## When to see a pharmacist or GP

Talk to your pharmacist or GP if:

- your medicine looks different and you're not sure it's the right one
- you notice new side effects after a change of brand
- you think you may have taken two products with the same active ingredient

If someone has taken the wrong medicine or too much, call the Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26.

**Call 000 in an emergency.** If someone has collapsed, isn't breathing or is having a severe allergic reaction, call triple zero (000) straight away.
