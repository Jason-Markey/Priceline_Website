Fragrance is one of the most common reasons skin reacts to beauty products. DermNet calls fragrances the most common source of contact dermatitis from cosmetics, and says fragrance allergy is second only to nickel as a cause of allergic contact dermatitis. If perfume leaves you itchy, red or wheezy, it's worth working out why. Depending on the cause, the answer might be changing how you wear scent, or avoiding fragrance altogether.

Our pharmacists and beauty team at Pacific Fair can help with both sides of that. This guide covers the signs of a reaction, how allergy is confirmed, what product labels really mean, how to wear and gift scent when skin is sensitive, and how fragrance can interact with sunshine and asthma.

## Is it an allergy or irritation?

Not every reaction to perfume is an allergy. DermNet describes several kinds of skin reaction to cosmetics, and they behave differently.

| Reaction | What it looks like | When it shows up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Irritant contact dermatitis | Redness, dryness or stinging where the product touched | Within minutes or hours for strong irritants; days to weeks for milder ones |
| Allergic contact dermatitis | An itchy, red, scaly rash, sometimes with swelling or blisters | Usually more than 12 hours after contact, peaking around 48 hours |
| Contact urticaria (hives) | Raised, itchy welts with burning or tingling | Within minutes to an hour, usually gone within 24 hours |
| Sun reaction (phytophotodermatitis) | Red skin and blisters, often in streaks, then brown marks | About 24–48 hours after sun exposure |

Irritant reactions are the most common type: DermNet says they account for 80% of all cases of contact dermatitis. healthdirect notes that with an allergic reaction, you'll usually only develop a rash a day or so after touching the allergen.

### What are the signs of a fragrance allergy?

According to DermNet, fragrance allergy often shows up as dermatitis in a streaky pattern where the product touched your skin. It's typically itchy and may burn or sting, with red, scaly patches, swelling or small blisters. In women, the hands, face and neck are most often affected; in men, the hands, face and lower legs. Armpits are commonly affected in both, so think about your deodorant too.

It doesn't have to be your own product, either. DermNet notes that reacting to a partner's fragrance (consort allergy) is well recognised, so it's worth thinking about the perfume, aftershave or moisturiser of the people closest to you.

## How do you find out if you're allergic to fragrance?

With patch testing. healthdirect explains that a doctor can diagnose allergic contact dermatitis using patch testing, which usually takes around 5 days, and may refer you to a dermatologist or an allergist. Your GP is the place to start.

DermNet says testing usually begins with fragrance mix I, fragrance mix II and balsam of Peru. Fragrance mix I combined with balsam of Peru picks up about 75% of people with a fragrance allergy, and fragrance mix II adds to that.

At home, DermNet suggests checking a new product by applying a small amount to your inner wrist or elbow and watching for a reaction over 24–48 hours. For people with a confirmed fragrance allergy, DermNet describes this kind of repeat open application test as a practical way to screen new products.

## "Fragrance-free" vs "unscented": what's the difference?

The labels sound similar but don't mean the same thing. According to DermNet:

- **Fragrance-free or without perfume:** generally means no fragrance or masking scent has been added, although DermNet notes some of these products could still include masking fragrances.
- **Unscented:** doesn't always mean fragrance-free. A fragrance material may have been added to mask the smell of other ingredients.
- **Hypoallergenic:** worth looking for, but reactions can still happen.
- **Natural, botanical or herbal:** products listing these ingredients may contain an unidentified fragrance.

The safest habit is to read the ingredient list, not just the front of the pack.

## How can you wear scent if your skin is sensitive?

If patch testing confirms a fragrance allergy, avoiding the allergen is the most reliable approach, and healthdirect notes that avoiding the trigger is the main treatment for contact dermatitis. That may mean going without perfume, so talk to your GP or dermatologist about what's safe for you.

If your skin is simply easily irritated, these tips can help:

- **Spray clothing, not skin.** Test a hidden patch of fabric first. Our tips on [making perfume last longer](/make-perfume-last-longer/) explain which fabrics to avoid.
- **Go lighter.** An eau de toilette or eau de cologne has a lower fragrance concentration than an eau de parfum, so there's less fragrance on you.
- **Keep it off damaged skin.** Avoid spraying onto broken, sunburnt or irritated skin.
- **Look after your skin barrier.** Use a fragrance-free moisturiser, especially if your skin is dry. Our [dry skin remedies](/dry-skin-remedies/) checklist can help.
- **Test before you commit.** Try a new product on your inner wrist or elbow for a day or two first.

### How do you choose a fragrance gift for someone with sensitive skin?

- **Ask first.** Find out quietly whether they have sensitive skin, eczema, asthma or a known fragrance allergy.
- **Go fragrance-free.** A fragrance-free pampering gift, such as a rich hand cream or body moisturiser, is a thoughtful alternative.
- **Think beyond perfume.** Candles, room sprays and diffusers can still bother someone whose asthma is triggered by scent.
- **Let them choose.** A gift card lets them pick something they know suits their skin.

## Can fragrance cause sun reactions or asthma?

### Can perfume react with the sun?

Yes. DermNet describes **berloque dermatitis**, a reaction that happens when skin is exposed first to perfume or eau de cologne and then to sunlight. It's a type of phytophotodermatitis, caused by natural plant chemicals called furanocoumarins, which are found in some citrus plants. It isn't an allergy, and DermNet says it can develop in anyone.

The skin turns red and often blisters, sometimes in streaks or drip shapes, about 24–48 hours after sun exposure. As it heals, it usually leaves brown marks that may last for years. On the Gold Coast, where UV reaches 3 or above every day, spray fragrance onto clothing or covered skin before a day outdoors. If you're already dealing with brown patches, see our guide to [pigmentation and melasma in Queensland](/pigmentation-melasma-queensland/).

### Can fragrance trigger asthma?

It can. healthdirect lists irritants such as perfumes and aerosol products among asthma triggers, and notes that avoiding or managing your triggers can help control asthma. If scent affects your breathing:

- choose non-aerosol products and apply them in a well-ventilated room
- ask family and workmates to go easy on strong scents around you
- keep your reliever with you and make sure your asthma action plan is up to date

## When to see a pharmacist or GP

Talk to a pharmacist if a product leaves your skin mildly red, dry or itchy. See a GP (our in-store [Help Medical GPs](/help-medical/) are one option) if:

- a rash is widespread, appears suddenly, is painful or gets worse when you use a product you were advised to try
- you have a fever or pus in blisters, which healthdirect says needs urgent medical attention
- a rash keeps coming back and you don't know the cause, so patch testing can be arranged
- your eyelids or face stay red and itchy
- you get blisters or brown marks after wearing perfume in the sun
- strong scents are triggering asthma symptoms

**Call 000 in an emergency.** DermNet notes severe allergic reactions (anaphylaxis) to cosmetics are rare but can be fatal, and usually start within an hour. If someone has swelling of the face, lips or tongue, or severe trouble breathing, call triple zero (000) immediately.
