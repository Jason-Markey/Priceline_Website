<p>Skincare for humid weather comes down to three things: lighter layers, gentle cleansing and daily sun protection. Gold Coast summers mean sweat, sticky afternoons and hours in air-conditioning, so the heavy routine that suited winter often feels too much by November. Below is a simple morning and night routine for oily, dry and sensitive skin, plus what to swap when the humidity rises.</p>

<p>If you'd like help matching products to your skin, our beauty advisors can take you through our <a href="/skincare-tanning/">skincare and sun care range</a> in store.</p>

<h2>How does humid weather affect your skin?</h2>

<h3>Sweat and trapped heat</h3>
<p>In humid weather, sweat doesn't evaporate as quickly, so skin stays damp for longer. Healthdirect explains that heat rash (also called prickly heat or miliaria) happens when sweat ducts get blocked and sweat builds up under the skin. It's more common in hot, humid weather and in babies, and it's more likely if you wear tight or heavy clothes that trap heat and moisture.</p>

<h3>Shine and breakouts</h3>
<p>Many people notice their skin looks shinier in summer, and sweat mixed with sunscreen, makeup and sand can leave skin feeling congested. Healthdirect suggests a gentle, soap-free, pH-balanced face wash twice a day, oil-free makeup removed every night, and not picking or squeezing pimples. It also warns that washing too often can make acne worse.</p>

<h3>Air-con whiplash</h3>
<p>Moving between muggy outdoor air and cool, dry air-con, whether at work, in the car or walking through Pacific Fair, can leave some people oily on the surface but tight underneath. That's often a sign your skin wants lighter hydration, not none at all.</p>

<h3>Pigmentation</h3>
<p>The Australasian College of Dermatologists lists sun exposure and hormonal changes (for example pregnancy or the contraceptive pill) among the factors that play a role in melasma, a type of patchy pigmentation. The College recommends sun protection as an essential part of managing it. Cancer Council Queensland notes the UV Index here reaches 3 or above every day, so daily sunscreen matters for uneven skin tone all year, not just in summer.</p>

<h2>What to swap for summer</h2>

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Winter habit</th><th>Summer swap</th></tr>
</thead>

<tr><td>Rich cream moisturiser</td><td>Lotion, gel-cream or fluid</td></tr>

<h2>A Gold Coast summer routine by skin type</h2>

<p>These are starting points, not rules. Introduce one new product at a time and patch test first, so you can tell what's working for you.</p>

<h3>Oily or breakout-prone skin</h3>
<p><strong>Morning</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Cleanse with a gentle, soap-free gel cleanser.</li>
<li>Apply a light, oil-free moisturiser, or skip it if your sunscreen feels hydrating enough.</li>
<li>Apply SPF50 or SPF50+ broad-spectrum sunscreen. A dry-touch or gel texture suits oily skin.</li>
<li>Carry blotting papers rather than piling on powder through the day.</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Night</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Remove sunscreen and makeup, using a cleanser or micellar water first if you wore makeup.</li>
<li>Cleanse again gently.</li>
<li>Apply a light, non-comedogenic moisturiser.</li>
<li>If breakouts are bothering you, ask your pharmacist which over-the-counter options may suit your skin.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Dry or dehydrated skin</h3>
<p><strong>Morning</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Rinse with water or use a creamy, soap-free cleanser.</li>
<li>Apply a hydrating serum if you like, then a lightweight lotion rather than a thick cream.</li>
<li>Apply SPF50 or SPF50+ broad-spectrum sunscreen. A lotion texture can double as extra moisture.</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Night</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Cleanse gently to remove sunscreen.</li>
<li>Use a richer moisturiser at night, when you're not sweating or out in the humidity.</li>
<li>Keep an eye on dry patches from air-con, and add a little extra moisturiser there.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Sensitive or easily flushed skin</h3>
<p><strong>Morning</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Rinse with lukewarm water or use a gentle, fragrance-free cleanser.</li>
<li>Apply a simple fragrance-free moisturiser.</li>
<li>Apply a fragrance-free SPF50 or SPF50+ broad-spectrum sunscreen. Cancer Council suggests people with sensitive skin look for fragrance-free and paraben-free products.</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Night</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Cleanse gently, patting rather than rubbing dry.</li>
<li>Apply the same simple moisturiser. Keep active ingredients to a minimum while your skin is hot or irritated.</li>
<li>Cool down before bed. A lukewarm shower and a fan can be kinder than a hot shower on a flushed face.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Where sunscreen fits in your routine</h2>

<p>Sunscreen is the step that matters most for a Gold Coast summer. Cancer Council says to apply it on clean, dry skin before moisturisers or makeup, 20 minutes before going outside, and to reapply every two hours and after swimming, sweating or towelling. If you'll be indoors most of the day, a cosmetic with SPF30 is adequate for incidental sun. For beach days, school pick-ups on the oval or an afternoon at the theme parks, use a dedicated SPF50 or SPF50+ sunscreen.</p>

<h2>Body skin in the humidity</h2>

<ul>
<li>Shower after the beach to rinse off salt, sand and sunscreen, then moisturise while your skin is still slightly damp.</li>
<li>Choose loose, breathable clothing to reduce trapped heat and sweat.</li>
<li>Keep skin folds clean and dry, and change out of wet swimwear.</li>
<li>If you use a self-tanner, exfoliate gently beforehand, and remember a tan from a bottle doesn't protect you from UV.</li>
</ul>

<h2>When to see a pharmacist or GP</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Heat rash</strong> usually settles if you keep cool and dry. Healthdirect advises seeing a doctor if it's severe, looks infected, keeps coming back, or doesn't improve after a few days in a cooler place.</li>
<li><strong>Acne</strong> that is bothering you, spreading beyond your face or leaving marks is worth discussing with your GP. Healthdirect notes untreated acne can scar. Your pharmacist can talk you through non-prescription options first.</li>
<li><strong>New patchy pigmentation</strong>, especially during pregnancy or after starting a new medicine, is worth checking with your GP.</li>
<li><strong>Any spot or mole</strong> that is new, changing or different should be checked by a GP. Our in-store <a href="/help-medical/">Help Medical GPs</a> are right here at Pacific Fair.</li>
<li><strong>Signs of heat illness</strong>, such as confusion or collapse, are a medical emergency. Call 000.</li>
</ul>
